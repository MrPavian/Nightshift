# Martha Cooper and the Photographic Archive of Graffiti 01

**Status:** source-led research dossier; publication and image-use rights remain item-specific.  
**Scope:** Martha Cooper's documentary role, photographic method, publications and the Martha Cooper Library at URBAN NATION.

## 1. Why Cooper belongs at the centre of the archive

Martha Cooper is not merely a source of attractive reference images. Her work is a major documentary record of New York graffiti and early hip-hop culture, and her practice raises a central historical question: how can an ephemeral public work survive after the wall or train has disappeared?

Cooper moved to New York in 1977 and worked as a staff photographer for the New York Post. During this period she began documenting graffiti and B-boying in the Bronx. Her collaboration with Henry Chalfant, *Subway Art*, was first published in 1984 and became a major international reference for graffiti writers. [MC-01; MC-02]

Cooper's own account stresses that she was introduced to the practice through a young writer showing her a notebook and explaining that he was practising to put his name on a wall. This matters for the archive: the photograph, the sketchbook and the person explaining the practice are connected forms of evidence, not interchangeable illustrations. [MC-02]

## 3. Publications to catalogue

Initial bibliography leads; edition details and holdings must be checked in the MCL catalogue and library catalogues.

- *Subway Art* — Martha Cooper and Henry Chalfant; first published 1984. Train photography and the visual/historical record of New York subway writing.
- *Hip Hop Files 1980–1984* — early hip-hop photography and cultural context.
- *Street Play* — young people and creative street culture.
- *Tag Town* — development of graffiti from tags to more elaborate pieces.
- *We B*Girlz* — women and girls in hip-hop culture; relevant to the archive's gender and representation work.
- *New York State of Mind* — Cooper's New York photography.
- *Going Postal* and *Name Tagging* — sticker/postal surfaces and name writing.
- *Tokyo Tattoo 1970* — a separate documentary project that illustrates the breadth of Cooper's photographic practice.

Do not assume that every edition or image is held by the Martha Cooper Library; verify catalogue records.

## 4. What photographs can and cannot prove

A photograph can document:
- a visible work at a particular moment;
- a location or surface, when reliably captioned;
- a writer, crew or event, when attribution is documented;
- visual relationships between letters, panels, train architecture and surroundings;
- the existence of an ephemeral work that may no longer survive.

A photograph alone cannot always prove:
- the exact date, writer or crew;
- the full train or wall composition if only one section is shown;
- original colour, scale, viewing conditions or the complete social context;
- the writer's intention or the meaning of a piece;
- permission to reproduce the photograph.

Captions, contact sheets, negatives/slides, photographer notes, oral histories and institutional records should be cross-referenced wherever possible.

## 5. NIGHTSHIFT visual-reference protocol

For every Cooper image considered:
1. record exact book/exhibition/archive source and page or object identifier;
2. capture the original caption verbatim in research notes where permitted, then paraphrase in game copy;
3. distinguish photographer, depicted writer, crew, location and date as separate fields;
4. record whether attribution is photographer-supplied, archive-supplied or later editorial;
5. set rights status independently from historical evidence status;
6. use reference-only material for analysis unless written permission or an applicable licence covers the intended use;
7. never trace a protected piece or convert a writer's signature into a generator preset.
