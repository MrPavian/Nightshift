# Visibility and gaps in the record

> Who was photographed, interviewed and collected is not the same as who took part. Women have written since the early decades; the record is uneven.

## Introduction

Based on fifteen years of research with more than 100 women in 23 countries, Jessica Nydia Pabón-Colón argues that the image of graffiti as a boys' club makes women writers disappear from public imagination [c1]. A review of gender in graffiti research notes that foundational studies described writers as predominantly male while also acknowledging women and girls from the beginning [c2].

## Why it matters

An archive built only from the most photographed figures repeats their omissions. Press histories, male narrators of crew networks and photographs without the maker's name can make a scene look more male than the evidence suggests [c3].

## How to recognize it

Useful questions for any entry: who was writing here, who was photographed or interviewed, which sources let a person speak for themselves, and who is missing from the surviving record [c3].

## Not the same as

- **A separate women's section** — women belong in the ordinary chronology of scenes, crews, styles and books, as in [[person.lady-pink|Lady Pink]]'s place in the New York history [c4].
