# New York: the emergence of modern writing

> How young people in New York turned the writing of names in public space into a recognisable, competitive culture with its own words, forms and audiences.

## Introduction

Modern New York writing did not appear from nowhere, and no single person invented it. During the late 1960s and early 1970s, young people transformed existing ways of writing names and initials in the city into deliberate, stylised writing [NYC-002]. Philadelphia developed a related but distinct name-writing culture in the same decade, so a simple one-city origin story does not hold [NYC-003].

By the early-to-mid 1970s a recognisable New York writing culture had formed, with its own terms, practices and peer recognition [NYC-004]. Writers increasingly developed personal handstyles and more elaborate letter compositions during the decade [NYC-006].

## Why it matters

This is the period in which the vocabulary that later travelled around the world took shape: [[practice.tag|tags]], larger compositions and the idea of style as a personal achievement. The subway turned that work into a city-wide display [NYC-007].

## Context and development

A 1971 New York Times story about TAKI 183 helped make the practice publicly visible, but it does not prove that he invented it [NYC-005]. Researchers describe a web of influences instead of one clean root [NYC-002].

Women took part from the early period. Lady Pink, for example, describes starting in 1979 and painting subway cars, and she was included in a 1980 exhibition at sixteen [c1]. Her work belongs in the main story of this period, not in a footnote [[person.lady-pink|(profile)]].

The history of writing and the history of the city's campaign against it are related but separate stories [NYC-008]; see [[history.clean-train-campaign|the Clean Train campaign]].

## Not the same as

- **The invention of graffiti** (→ concept.public-inscription) — public inscription is far older and has many separate histories; this chapter is about one modern writing culture.
- **A single inventor** — press fame, as in the 1971 TAKI 183 story, is not proof of invention [NYC-005].
