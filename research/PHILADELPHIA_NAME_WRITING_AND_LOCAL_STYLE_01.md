# Philadelphia Name Writing and Local Style — Research Dossier 01

**Document type:** Regional historical dossier / style research  
**Status:** Source-backed synthesis; chronology and attribution remain open to further oral-history verification  
**Scope:** Philadelphia, c. 1960s–present, with emphasis on the emergence of modern name writing and local handstyle traditions  
**Archive role:** A distinct regional chapter in the history of modern graffiti writing; not merely a preface to New York.

## 1. Executive finding

Philadelphia belongs near the beginning of the history of modern U.S. name-centred graffiti writing. During the 1960s, young people in the city wrote assumed names and nicknames across neighbourhood streets and transit corridors. Darryl McCray, known as CORNBREAD, became the best-known early figure, but the scene also included writers such as COOL EARL, TOPCAT 126 and others. The evidence does not justify reducing the emergence to a single inventor or one uncontested date.

Philadelphia and New York should be presented as related but distinct early centres. New York's subway system, scale of media documentation and later international circulation made its visual language globally dominant. Philadelphia retained a more locally specific name-writing culture, including long-form handstyles, tall hand / tall print and the highly distinctive WICKED / WICKET tradition. These are not simply early or inferior versions of New York styles.

The most important editorial correction is this: **Philadelphia is not just where a famous first tag supposedly happened. It is a scene with its own history, local vocabulary, visual grammar, social memory and continuing practice.**

## 2. What the evidence supports

### 2.1 Emergence in the 1960s

Heidi Pfeiffenberger's 2017 KIT dissertation places the emergence of contemporary graffiti writing in Philadelphia in the late 1950s / early 1960s and discusses CORNBREAD's activity around 1965 in North Philadelphia. She also identifies COOL EARL and TOPCAT 126 in the city from 1966 onward. The dissertation traces the growth of a city-wide scene between 1967 and 1969.

Menika Dirkson's article for Picturing Black History adds a detailed public-history account of Black teenagers who called themselves wall writers and situates the movement between approximately 1965 and 1985. It documents CORNBREAD's use of marker and spray paint, his city-wide visibility campaign and the way press coverage and police response shaped public perceptions.

Tyson Mitman's The Art of Defiance is a scholarly ethnographic history of Philadelphia graffiti. Its publisher record explicitly cautions against a simple gang-origin narrative and describes graffiti writing as a culture with its own rules and practices. Its chapter structure includes a dedicated discussion of the Philadelphia-specific WICKED tradition.

**Editorial position:** say that Philadelphia was one of the earliest documented centres of modern name-writing in the United States, with a particularly important emergence in the mid-to-late 1960s. Avoid an absolute “the first graffiti writer ever” claim.

### 2.2 Names, nicknames and social identity

Philadelphia's early names often came from nicknames, personal traits or local social relationships. CORNBREAD's name is associated in later accounts with a nickname acquired at the Youth Development Center. Other names identified in the research include COOL EARL, TOPCAT 126, KOOL KLEPTO KIDD and CHEWY.

François Chastanet's 2024/25 research on Philadelphia handstyles notes a contrast with the New York practice of appending a street number to a name. Philadelphia writers more often used nicknames or names grounded in personal identity. This is a useful comparative tendency, not an absolute rule for every writer.

### 2.3 From simple signatures to local letterform identity

Pfeiffenberger describes early CORNBREAD and COOL EARL tags as initially close to ordinary handwriting. Over time, writers developed more recognisable personal forms. The dissertation identifies elongated letters with lower “platforms” or base extensions as a characteristic Philadelphia feature. It also discusses TOPCAT 126's letter style and its later association in New York with “Broadway Elegant” / Manhattan Style.

This is an important cross-city link, but it should be presented carefully. The claim that TOPCAT 126 directly carried a particular Philadelphia style into New York is supported by Pfeiffenberger's synthesis of earlier literature; it is not a fully documented step-by-step transmission record. The chronology of his move and the exact nature of the stylistic transfer remain matters for further corroboration.

## 3. Philadelphia's distinctive visual traditions

### 3.1 Gangster Hand / early Philadelphia hand

Pfeiffenberger describes a local early style characterised by elongated letters and “platforms” at the lower ends of letterforms. The style was more than a personal flourish: repeated visual conventions helped create a recognisable local texture.

Do not treat “Gangster Hand” as a universal or timeless category. It is a historically situated label found in particular research and scene accounts. Preserve alternate local terminology and source attribution.

### 3.2 Tall Hand / Tall Print

The Philadelphia tradition includes large, vertically extended name-writing that uses as much of a wall or available surface as the writer can reach. Chastanet's documentation describes tall forms as part of the city's distinct handstyle culture. Mitman's book is also a key source for the social meaning of locally specific writing.

This should be taught as a lettering and spatial practice, not reduced to “a very tall tag”. The relationship between letter proportions, available surface, reach, repetition and the visual field matters.

### 3.3 Wicked / Wicket

The WICKED or WICKET is a strongly Philadelphia-associated handstyle tradition. It is described in Mitman's book as a local style with a high degree of insider recognition. Chastanet's work describes the local vocabulary of “whip” and the flowing, connected energy of the hand. The Haverford College exhibition ALL BIG LETTERS characterises Wickeds as exaggerated, cryptic and elongated tags, often designed to be read and appreciated by members of the writing community rather than the general public.

Do not flatten these sources into a single technical definition. They describe overlapping but distinct dimensions:
- a local style tradition and identity marker;
- elongated, angular or highly stylised letterforms;
- a relationship to the writer's gesture and connected movement;
- insider legibility and community recognition;
- a living tradition with individual variation.

### 3.4 “Whip” as a movement concept

Chastanet's field research describes “whip” as a Philadelphia term for the energy instilled in letter strokes: continuity, speed, consistency, joints and ligatures, wrist movement and the visual trace of a flowing hand.

This is valuable for NIGHTSHIFT's handstyle research, but must remain attributed to Chastanet's 2023 field study and the local practitioners he engaged. It is not a universal graffiti term.

**Design inference for NIGHTSHIFT:** use the concept to study stroke continuity, rhythm, repeated directional movement and joins in original synthetic lettering. Do not reproduce a named Philadelphia writer's signature or generate a WICKED preset from a real tag.

## 4. Philadelphia and New York: connection without hierarchy

The archive should resist two oversimplifications:
1. “New York invented modern graffiti and exported it to the world.”
2. “Philadelphia invented everything and New York merely copied it.”

The better-supported picture is more nuanced:
- Philadelphia and New York both developed name-writing practices during the 1960s.
- Philadelphia has early documented writers and a distinct local handstyle tradition.
- New York developed a much larger and more visible subway-writing culture during the 1970s and 1980s.
- Writers, travel, neighbourhood networks and media connected the cities.
- TOPCAT 126 is identified in Pfeiffenberger's dissertation as an important Philadelphia-to-New York stylistic connection, particularly in relation to Broadway Elegant.
- The exact chronology and mechanics of influence should remain qualified unless supported by additional first-person and contemporary records.

The global visibility of New York writing later obscured the distinctiveness of Philadelphia's earlier and continuing scene. Chastanet explicitly argues that the global prominence of New York's visual archetypes has hidden a more complex history.

## 5. Social context: avoid the gang-only explanation

Philadelphia writing developed in a city where gang markings and territorial signs were part of the urban landscape. But scholarly and public-history sources caution against treating all early writers as gang members or all writing as gang communication.

Mitman's ethnographic work specifically challenges the reduction of Philadelphia graffiti to gang origins and examines the way writing allowed participants to build social identity, community and a public voice. Dirkson's account also distinguishes teenagers who chose writing from gang activity, while showing that newspapers and authorities often conflated graffiti and violence.

The archive should explain the overlap in urban context without treating the practices as identical.

## 8. Visual research and rights

Useful visual sources:
- Heidi Pfeiffenberger's dissertation includes period examples and analysis of early Philadelphia tags and letterforms. Academic access does not automatically grant image reuse rights.
- Chastanet's project includes contemporary street documentation and first-person hand sessions. Treat the images and videos as research references unless a reuse license or written permission is confirmed.
- Haverford College's ALL BIG LETTERS exhibition provides a curated interpretive context and documented image captions. Check each item's rights separately.
- Picturing Black History includes archival photographs credited to Getty Images. These are not automatically reusable.

For NIGHTSHIFT 1.0, original explanatory diagrams should teach:
1. elongated letter proportions and lower platforms;
2. the difference between Tall Hand and a conventional tag scaled vertically;
3. a neutral stroke-flow diagram inspired by the documented concept of whip, not copied from a living or historical writer;
4. the distinction between a local style tradition and a single writer's personal hand.

## 9. Research gaps

1. Find and inspect primary Philadelphia newspaper coverage from 1965–1972, including the July 25, 1971 New York Times coverage that described Philadelphia as the “graffiti capital”.
2. Collect oral histories from CORNBREAD, COOL EARL, TOPCAT 126 and other early participants where available; attribute memories and disagreements.
3. Verify TOPCAT 126's move to New York and the exact chronology of his interaction with early NYC writers.
4. Identify and verify the early Philadelphia clubs/crews and their membership histories.
5. Establish when and how the terms “Gangster Hand”, “Tall Hand”, “Tall Print”, “Wicked” and “Wicket” were used by local writers.
6. Examine Philadelphia transit and trolley routes as a distinct circulation infrastructure; do not assume the NYC subway model.
7. Document women writers and underrepresented participants in Philadelphia's early and contemporary scenes.
8. Add a visual corpus with item-level dates, locations, attributions and image rights.

## 10. Sources

### S1 — Heidi Franziska Pfeiffenberger, Style Writing. Skripturale Ästhetik zwischen Text und Bild am Beispiel von Karlsruhe und Mannheim (KIT dissertation, 2017)
A doctoral dissertation with a dedicated history of American and European style writing. Pages 73–76 discuss Philadelphia's emergence, CORNBREAD, COOL EARL, TOPCAT 126, elongated letterforms with platforms, the Wicked tradition, and the claimed connection between TOPCAT 126 and Broadway Elegant in New York.

- Full PDF: https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000079409/6209138
- Relevant printed pages: 73–76.
- Evidence class: doctoral dissertation.
- Limitation: historical synthesis relies on a range of earlier sources, including some specialist publications and retrospective accounts; verify important origin and transmission claims independently.

### S2 — Tyson Mitman, The Art of Defiance: Graffiti, Politics and the Reimagined City in Philadelphia (Intellect, 2018)
Ethnographic study of Philadelphia graffiti writing, its cultural history, social identity and local style traditions. The publisher/JSTOR record describes the work as a corrective to simplistic gang-origin narratives. The table of contents includes “Kick It Wicked”, a dedicated section on the local style.

- JSTOR: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv36xw37f
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/j.ctv36xw37f
- Evidence class: scholarly ethnographic monograph.
- Limitation: full text access is restricted in the currently available record; consult the book for detailed claims and page references before final publication.

### S3 — Menika Dirkson, “The Graffiti Art Movement in Philadelphia,” Picturing Black History
Public-history article describing Black youth writing culture in Philadelphia from the 1960s to 1980s, CORNBREAD, early names, press coverage, police response and later mural programmes.

- https://picturingblackhistory.org/the-graffiti-art-movement-in-philadelphia/
- Evidence class: public history with a source list.
- Limitation: secondary synthesis; attribute detailed recollections and dates to the sources used.

### S4 — François Chastanet, “The Survival of The Whip” (2024 report; expanded online edition 2025)
Field-based visual and gestural research on contemporary Philadelphia handstyles, including local terminology, “whip”, transit corridors, the persistence of name writing and the relationship between Philadelphia and New York.

- https://francoischastanet.com/philadelphia-handstyles/
- Evidence class: specialist field research and visual documentation.
- Limitation: the author's interpretation and selected field observations are not a complete census; practitioner terminology should be attributed.

### S5 — RJ Rushmore, ALL BIG LETTERS, Haverford College Cantor Fitzgerald Gallery (2017)
Exhibition and essay explaining modern name-writing, Philadelphia and New York, the insider audience for graffiti and the local meaning of Wickeds.

- https://exhibits.haverford.edu/allbigletters/
- Evidence class: college gallery exhibition and curatorial essay.
- Limitation: curatorial interpretation; not a substitute for scholarly or primary historical evidence.

### S6 — David Ley and Roman Cybriwsky, “Urban Graffiti as Territorial Markers,” Annals of the Association of American Geographers 64(4), 1974, pp. 491–505
Contemporary urban-geography research on territorial graffiti in Philadelphia. Useful for understanding the urban context in which name writing emerged, but should not be used to collapse all writing into gang or territorial marking.

- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8306.1974.tb0099
- Evidence class: peer-reviewed contemporary research.
- Limitation: concerns territorial marking; it does not represent the full later writing culture.
