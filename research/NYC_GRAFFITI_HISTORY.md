# New York Graffiti History — Research Notes

> Scope: the emergence and development of modern New York writing, especially the late 1960s through the 1990s. This is a working chronology, not a single-origin story.

## 1. Before the subway era: names, neighborhoods and early writing (late 1960s–early 1970s)

New York writing did not appear fully formed as the elaborate subway pieces later documented in photographs. Joe Austin describes young people in the 1960s and early 1970s transforming the older practice of writing initials and names on neighborhood walls into a more deliberately designed form. Practitioners called the activity **writing**, while critics and public institutions commonly called it **graffiti**. Austin situates this development within a city saturated by commercial names, billboards, neon, magazines, newspapers and comic-book typography. [A]

The Bronx County Historical Society's oral-history collection documents early writers and scenes in the late 1960s and early 1970s, including ROCKY 184's recollections of the Writer's Corner in Washington Heights and the early activity of STAFF 161 and the Ebony Dukes. These oral histories are valuable first-person testimony, not a reason to declare one uncontested inventor or birthplace. [A]

A SCHIRN Kunsthalle overview reports that the 1971 New York Times article about TAKI 183 helped amplify the practice across the city. It also describes the early competition around frequency and placement, followed by individual stylistic differentiation. Treat the newspaper story as an important accelerator in this account, not as proof that writing began with TAKI 183. [B]

### Design implications
- Model the early phase as a spread of name-writing practices, not a sudden invention by one person.
- Show tags/handstyles as authored signatures with individual rhythm and letterform choices, not simply default text in a handwriting font.
- Preserve the distinction between the practitioners' term **writing** and the external umbrella term **graffiti**.

## 2. From tags to more elaborate lettering (early–mid 1970s)

As writing spread, competition was not only about how often a name appeared or where it was placed. Writers increasingly developed recognizable personal lettering. The SCHIRN account describes LEE163d! using intertwined, curved letters on the fronts of trains, influencing others. It also identifies the **signature-piece** as an intermediate form: in early 1972, some writers thickened their signatures and added a contrasting outline. [B]

The Bronx oral-history collection records the importance of early crews and individual recollections. It describes STAFF 161 as founder of the Ebony Dukes and RIFF 170 as a member of INDS, one of the early Bronx crews; these are collection descriptions and should be attributed as such rather than generalized into an absolute ranking. [A]

### Design implications
- Treat tag, signature-piece and larger piece as related but distinct stages/forms, not as a rigid universal evolutionary ladder.
- Build the handstyle as a coherent signature system: stroke rhythm, slant, joins, spacing and terminal behavior.
- A transition from tag to piece should add mass, outline and composition, not merely scale up the same font.

## 3. Subway writing and the photographic record (mid-1970s–1980s)

The subway became a major moving exhibition surface. Henry Chalfant's photographs document works that no longer exist and preserve train-length compositions through sequences of overlapping photographs. The Bronx Museum describes his 1977 technique of photographing from multiple platform positions to capture entire trains. Its exhibition material identifies writers including Dondi, Futura, Lady Pink, Lee Quiñones, Zephyr, Blade, Crash, DAZE, SEEN, Skeme and T-Kid in this historical record. [A]

The Museum of the City of New York's Martin Wong collection documents another crucial transition: writers moving from subway cars into paintings on canvas and exhibitions in galleries and museums. The collection includes more than 300 canvases and works on paper, with work by artists such as Rammellzee, Lee Quiñones, Lady Pink and Futura 2000. [A]

Joe Austin's *Taking the Train* places the writing culture within the political, institutional and public-relations conflicts surrounding New York's transit system. His account cautions against reducing the era to a purely stylistic story: the history also involves municipal policy, public space, youth culture and the city's campaign against subway writing. [A]

### Design implications
- Subway-car compositions need their own format logic: panels, doors, windows, car segmentation, long horizontal flow and train-wide sequencing.
- A whole-train composition is not simply a large wall piece; the vehicle's architecture is part of the design problem.
- The archive should show both the artwork and its documentation context. A photograph is evidence of a now-vanished work, not necessarily the work's complete original appearance.

## 4. Erasure, transition and survival beyond the trains (mid-1980s–1990s)

The disappearance of large-scale subway writing from the transit fleet did not mean that writing culture disappeared. Austin traces how the culture survived the city's campaign and became an international art movement and an important part of hip-hop culture. The MCNY collection and exhibitions document artists moving into canvases, paper, galleries and museums. [A]

Do not represent this as a simple end date. Different practices, cities, surfaces and communities developed at different rates. The history of New York subway painting and the wider history of graffiti writing overlap but are not identical.

### Design implications
- The game's archive timeline should not end at the "clean train" moment.
- Show changing surfaces, audiences, institutions and circulation of style.
- Avoid implying that gallery recognition replaced or invalidated street-based practice.

## 5. Evidence cautions and unresolved questions

- Origins are multiple and contested. Oral histories should be attributed to narrators and interviewers.
- The term "graffiti" is often an external label; many practitioners prefer "writing" or "graffiti writing" in particular contexts.
- Historical style names can be retrospective, local or crew-associated. Verify each term's use before presenting it as universal.
- A photograph's date, location, train line and writer attribution must be checked against the archive record; captions are not interchangeable.
- Further research needed: borough/neighborhood differences; women writers and under-documented participants; crew networks; the changing role of walls after subway cleaning; European and global transmission; legal/illegal distinctions and commissioned work.
