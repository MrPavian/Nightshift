# NYC Writing in the 1990s: Borough and Scene Map 01

**Document type:** Borough-level research synthesis  
**Status:** Evidence-led starter map; uneven coverage is intentional  
**Scope:** New York City, 1990–1999  
**Purpose:** Translate documented locations, networks and media into a cautious spatial framework for NIGHTSHIFT.

## 1. Method and limits

This is not a complete borough census. The available sources are uneven: the Bronx is represented by detailed practitioner accounts and institutional oral histories; downtown Manhattan by retrospective reporting on IRAK; Queens by the archive and publishing work of Greg Lamarche / Sp.One; Brooklyn appears in specific cross-borough networks and landmark writing, but the current evidence does not support a complete Brooklyn crew map.

A person's residence, school, meeting place, crew affiliation and painting territory are different kinds of geography. Do not collapse them into one “home borough” field. Likewise, a photograph's location does not automatically prove the writer's usual territory.

Use four labels:
- **Documented location:** a source explicitly identifies a place or borough.
- **Scene association:** a writer or group is associated with a borough/network, but exact territorial boundaries are unclear.
- **Media node:** a publication, archive, studio or meeting point that connected writers beyond its immediate neighbourhood.
- **Research lead:** a plausible connection needing further corroboration.

## Additional legacy figure: SEEN / United Artists

SEEN (Richard “Richie” Mirando) is a major Bronx legacy figure whose career bridges the subway era and the 1990s. He began painting New York subway trains in 1973 with United Artists (UA), was associated with whole-car work, and continued painting trains until 1989 according to gallery biographies. His late-1980s Tattoo SEEN business, canvas work and international exhibitions provide a documented continuity into the 1990s beyond train painting. Treat him as a legacy / continuing-career figure, not as proof of a new-generation 1990s street crew.

## 2. Bronx — multiple generations and distinct networks

### 2.1 The post-subway generation

Sacha Jenkins' account describes Spek beginning to write in 1993, after the last painted subway car left service in May 1989. The younger generation built visibility on streets and rooftops alongside elevated train lines. Jenkins places Spek, Jee and COPE 2 in a specific Bronx network, linking Jee and Spek to BTV / BTC and COPE 2 to Kings Destroy (KD).

The 1995–96 email interview with Swatch (Art Crimes, interview conducted in 1995 and published 1996) gives another first-person view of Bronx social geography. Swatch describes attending Theodore Roosevelt High School in the Bronx, joining the Desperados, later forming The Rebels Movement (TRM) / The Roosevelt Mob, and being introduced to the wider writing scene by Kace, Kraze and Tru2. He names KD, TFO, TFT, UMXS, OTB and TPT among crews he had been associated with. His account is a personal testimony and should not be converted into a verified year-by-year roster.

### 2.2 Institutional memory and older crews

The Bronx County Historical Society's Bronx Aerosol Arts Documentary Project records oral histories with writers including BG 183, T-KID 170, SEN 1, REE, OLGA, SCRATCH, DOSE MPC and others. The collection is valuable for tracing long-running Bronx lineages and for identifying interviewees whose memories can be cross-checked against contemporary material.

The archive's stated purpose is to document early Bronx aerosol-art history; not every interview is specifically about the 1990s. Use individual interview descriptions and recordings before attributing a 1990s event to a person.

### 2.3 NIGHTSHIFT use

- Treat the Bronx as several overlapping networks, not one unified faction.
- Elevated-line rooftops and street visibility are historically grounded settings for the post-subway generation in Jenkins' account.
- Use school, neighbourhood, crew, friendship and mentorship as separate relationship types.
- Do not present the KD / BTV / BTC conflict described by Jenkins as representative of all Bronx writers.

## 3. Manhattan — downtown networks, legacy institutions and landmark writing

### 3.1 Downtown / Lower Manhattan

Noah Johnson's 2021 GQ reconstruction places the emergence of IRAK in the downtown skate and youth scene in the late 1990s. It describes EARSNOT / Kunle Martins, WAK STF and REHAB, and the gradual adoption of the IRAK name. By 1999, the article reports IRAK tags appearing widely downtown.

The article locates social nodes in and around Astor Place, Washington Square Park, SoHo, the Lower East Side and East Village. It also describes a broader circle involving skateboarders, ravers, artists, photographers and people who did not themselves write graffiti. Ryan McGinley is explicitly described as a photographer and close associate who did not write.

This matters for NIGHTSHIFT: a crew's social network can be wider than its set of active writers. Keep formal writer membership distinct from friends, documentarians, collaborators and hangout regulars.

### 3.2 Uptown / Washington Heights and the wider city

The Bronx County Historical Society's collection includes an oral history with ROCKY 184, associated with Writers Corner 188 in Washington Heights. The collection is primarily focused on the late 1960s through early 1980s, so it establishes a historical place-memory, not a continuous 1990s crew roster.

### 3.3 Landmark writing and hardcore-punk crossover

A 2017 i-D feature based on Freddy Alva's research for *Urban Styles: Graffiti in New York Hardcore* describes SANE and SMITH as brothers from uptown Manhattan. It reports a major SANE/SMITH production on the Brooklyn Bridge in 1990, and collaborations with hardcore writers HUSH, JERE and NATZ. The same article discusses DMS-affiliated MQ / MKUE and the movement of writing onto bridges, highways and streets after the subway campaign.

This is useful evidence for a New York writing scene that intersected with hardcore punk, but the article is retrospective and relies on a specialist book/interview tradition. Seek the book and dated photographs before turning these into detailed event records.

## 4. Queens — publishing, neighbourhood practice and the pre-internet archive

Greg Lamarche / Sp.One's 2026 Letterform Archive essay is unusually useful because it is a practitioner recollection linked to a substantial surviving archive.

Lamarche describes growing up in Forest Hills, Queens, with graffiti in his schoolyard and neighbourhood. He created *Skills*, which ran seven issues between 1992 and 1995 and received submissions from writers around the world. The archive includes letters, envelopes, flyers, photographs and material intended for an eighth issue that was never published.

The essay identifies several important media nodes:
- **Skills:** a zine with international submissions and a surviving personal archive.
- **G.S.E.L.:** valued by Lamarche for rare photographs from across New York City.
- **Styles for Miles (SFM):** especially associated with sketches and outlines.
- **Twelve Ounce Prophet:** described as building a following and later becoming one of the early online graffiti forums.
- **Videograf:** a graffiti zine reimagined as VHS video, with San 2, Kool Spin, William Green / Nic One and Sacha Jenkins credited as collaborators/producers.

The essay describes mail correspondence continuing through 1997, with email gradually replacing physical mail. It also records Dash Snow / Sacer as a teenage correspondent around 1995 who photographed street graffiti and referenced Tracy 168.

### NIGHTSHIFT use

Queens should not be represented merely as a peripheral residential borough. It is documented here as a source of neighbourhood writing culture and as a node in the media/archive network. *Skills* offers a strong model for an in-game zine and correspondence system.

## 5. Brooklyn — important connections, incomplete local map

The current source set supports several specific Brooklyn-related facts, but not a reliable full borough chronology for 1990–1999:

- The Brooklyn Writers Bench at Atlantic Avenue and the Brooklyn Bridge bench are described in the Writers Bench Collection's retrospective account of the earlier bench network. The same source explains how the 149th Street bench connected Bronx and Brooklyn writers through lines serving both boroughs. This is a historical network context, not proof that the benches operated with the same role throughout the 1990s.
- The i-D article reports SANE/SMITH's Brooklyn Bridge production in 1990. This is a landmark event lead, not a borough-wide crew map.
- GQ's IRAK account says Martins' family moved to East New York, Brooklyn, before the downtown network took shape. That is biographical geography, not evidence that IRAK was a Brooklyn-based crew.
- Jenkins' account of the Bronx networks includes references to subway-line and yard geography connecting boroughs; the exact locations and activity need individual verification.

**Research gap:** a Brooklyn-specific 1990s register needs contemporary local reporting, dated photographs, zine issues, oral histories and named writer testimony. Do not fill the gap by assigning citywide writers to Brooklyn based on one event or residence.

## 7. Design implications: build a living scene map, not borough stereotypes

1. **Represent nodes and routes.** A writer can live in one borough, paint in another, meet at a third place and circulate photographs internationally.
2. **Make media nodes playable.** Zines, letters, photo exchanges, VHS and later web forums are not decorative lore; they can drive discovery, reputation and historical memory.
3. **Differentiate network relationships.** Crew membership, friendship, mentorship, rivalry, collaboration, photography and publication are separate edges.
4. **Preserve temporal change.** A meeting point or crew may be important in one period and less relevant later.
5. **Use fictional composites for gameplay.** Real historical people and crews should appear only where the record supports them; fictional characters can populate gaps without false attribution.
6. **Avoid borough caricatures.** No borough should have one exclusive lettering style, practice or social identity.
7. **Treat the archive as partial.** The sources disproportionately preserve visible, published and interviewed participants; undocumented writers and local networks are underrepresented.

## 8. Research priorities

### High priority
- Obtain and inspect *Subscription to Mischief: Graffiti Zines of the 1990s* / Greg Lamarche's Skills archive material, subject to access and rights.
- Inspect individual Bronx Aerosol Arts Documentary Project interviews, prioritising BG 183, SEN 1, DOSE MPC, OLGA and SCRATCH for explicit 1990s recollections.
- Verify SANE/SMITH 1990 Brooklyn Bridge material through dated photographs and the *Urban Styles* book.
- Build a Brooklyn-specific oral-history and zine issue list before adding crews.

### Medium priority
- Identify issue dates, contributors and contents for G.S.E.L., Styles for Miles and Twelve Ounce Prophet.
- Cross-check Swatch's named crews against other first-person accounts and dated magazine material.
- Map exact public locations only where source records support them.

**Research conclusion:** The best-supported 1990s map is not a neat borough-by-borough roster. It is a network map: Bronx peer and crew networks; Manhattan downtown's late-1990s hybrid social scene; Queens' zine and correspondence infrastructure; and Brooklyn as a significant but under-documented node requiring further primary research.
