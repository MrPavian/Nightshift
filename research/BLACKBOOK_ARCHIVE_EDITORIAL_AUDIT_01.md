# BLACKBOOK ARCHIVE — Editorial Audit of Draft Entries 01–06

**Audit date:** 2026-11-15  
**Scope:** First six article drafts in `research/BLACKBOOK_ARCHIVE_ENTRY_DRAFTS_01.md` and `research/BLACKBOOK_ARCHIVE_ENTRY_DRAFTS_02.md`.  
**Status:** Independent editorial audit; not final publication approval.  
**Method:** Compare each draft's claims and bibliographic notes against the cited source records and available source text. This audit does not replace page-level verification of every remaining claim.

## Executive assessment

The six drafts have a sound editorial foundation. They generally avoid simplistic origin myths, preserve local variation and separate historical description from NIGHTSHIFT's product taxonomy. Their main weakness is not prose quality but uneven evidence granularity: several claims cite a broad bibliography rather than the exact source passage; some institutional or retrospective accounts are used without enough framing; and a few source descriptions overstate what the source itself establishes.

**Recommendation:** Keep all six as the first editorial batch, but do not mark any approved yet. Apply the corrections below, complete the named source checks, then pass the entries through a second copy-edit and structured-record validation.

## Cross-entry findings

### Strengths to preserve
- No single-inventor explanation for modern name-writing.
- Philadelphia and New York are treated as related but distinct histories.
- The subway's 1989 clean-fleet milestone is not presented as the end of graffiti culture.
- Women's participation is treated as a structural archival question, not merely a token biography.
- Regional circulation is described as adaptation rather than one-way copying.
- Blackbooks are framed as working and social objects, not just collections of finished drawings.
- Image rights are kept separate from historical evidence.

### Systemic corrections required
1. Replace broad source labels such as “Austin; Coetsee; institutional collections” with stable source IDs and exact page/section references in the claim ledger.
2. Distinguish source type: peer-reviewed scholarship, dissertation, first-person oral history, institutional interpretation, practitioner essay and retrospective publication.
3. Do not describe a source as a census or representative survey when it is a situated study or selected archive.
4. Keep date types separate: event date, recollection date, publication date, photo date and archive date.
5. Every claim about priority, influence, first/earliest, naming or transmission needs a source-specific attribution and explicit qualification.
6. Add the relevant local terminology in original language and explain translations/variants rather than silently normalizing them.
7. Keep visual rights independent: an image being hosted by a museum, university or artist does not automatically grant reuse permission.
8. In the final archive, each article should have a visible short source list and expandable claim-level evidence, not an unstructured bibliography dump.

## Entry 01 — The emergence of modern name-writing
**ID:** `history.modern-name-writing`  
**Assessment:** Strong foundation; minor-to-moderate historical framing and source traceability work remains.  
**Decision:** Retain, revise and source-check.

### What works
- Scope is explicitly modern name-centred writing, not all public inscription.
- Philadelphia and New York are both present without a winner-takes-all origin story.
- The 1971 TAKI 183 press event is correctly distinguished from invention.
- The text rejects a universal linear evolution from tags to throw-ups to pieces.

### Required changes
- The opening says “young people in several American cities” but the body and claim ledger chiefly document Philadelphia and New York. Either add specific, well-sourced third-city examples or narrow the sentence to the two documented early centres.
- MNE-02 should carry exact references to Pfeiffenberger's historical chapter and the specific Philadelphia source passages. Pfeiffenberger's dissertation identifies COOL EARL and TOPCAT 126 appearing in the city from 1966 and discusses the 1967–69 formation of a city-wide scene; keep this attributed to that study, not universal consensus.
- MNE-04 needs the direct 21 July 1971 New York Times source in the source list, not merely “contemporary New York Times article”. Use the original article record, and keep its role as a media event distinct from an origin claim.
- MNE-05 is broad synthesis. Break it into smaller, region- and period-bounded claims or retain it explicitly as an editorial synthesis supported by named sources.
- The phrase “over time, writers developed increasingly differentiated handstyles, larger letter compositions and shared vocabularies” is acceptable as a broad orientation but should link to later entries rather than imply one universal sequence.

### Source notes
- Pfeiffenberger (2017), especially historical chapter pages 70–76, is a strong secondary synthesis but should be attributed as such.
- The 1971 New York Times article should be linked directly: https://www.nytimes.com/1971/07/21/archives/taki-183-spawns-pen-pals.html
- Keep Joe Austin and Coetsee as contextual scholarship; cite precise pages for subway infrastructure and circulation claims.

## Entry 02 — Philadelphia: name-writing and local lettering
**ID:** `scene.philadelphia`  
**Assessment:** Valuable and distinctive; terminology needs better primary/local grounding.  
**Decision:** Retain, expand source precision and revise the terminology paragraph.

### What works
- Philadelphia is not presented as a primitive version of New York.
- The article foregrounds local letter traditions and local vocabulary.
- It correctly avoids asserting a universal “first writer” claim.
- It acknowledges that TOPCAT 126 influence claims need qualification.

### Required changes
- Use François Chastanet's 2024 “The Survival of The Whip” as a major contemporary specialist source for Tall Hands and Wickeds, including observed letterform and gesture analysis. Record the publication context: *States*, issue 2, Villa Albertine; the web version is augmented. Do not cite it merely as “research 2024/25”.
- Add the Haverford College / RJ Rushmore “ALL BIG LETTERS” source as an accessible interpretive source on the local cultural meaning of Wickeds. It describes Wickeds as quintessential Philadelphia tags and an insider-readable local tradition. It is not a substitute for practitioner testimony or specialist typographic analysis.
- Pfeiffenberger's dissertation (2017), pp. 73–76, describes PHILLY'S WICKED as a crew and the elongated, energetic letters associated with it; the draft should not conflate the crew name, the wider wicked tradition and every later use of “wicked”.
- Chastanet uses “Wickeds” and notes “Wickets” as a Philadelphia-accented variant. Preserve this as attributed terminology, not as a universal spelling rule.
- The current phrase “Gangster Hand”, “Tall Hand”, “Tall Print”, “Wicked” and “Wicket” should not be collapsed into one taxonomy is sound, but explain their relationships more precisely: Tall Hands and Wickeds are related but distinguishable local handstyle traditions; “Gangster Hand/Print” is a historically reported label; “Wicked/Wicket” has spelling/pronunciation variation.
- The TOPCAT 126 → Broadway Elegant / Manhattan Style statement should be attributed specifically to Pfeiffenberger's synthesis and treated as a reported influence narrative, not proven direct transmission. Avoid causal certainty unless a first-person account or stronger corroboration is added.
- Add source page numbers to the claim ledger, especially for the TOPCAT 126 and PHILLY'S WICKED discussion.

### Source notes
- Pfeiffenberger (2017), pp. 73–76: https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000079409/6209138
- François Chastanet, “The Survival of The Whip”: https://francoischastanet.com/philadelphia-handstyles/
- Haverford College, “ALL BIG LETTERS”: https://exhibits.haverford.edu/allbigletters/

## Entry 03 — Women in the writing scene: participation, visibility and the archive
**ID:** `history.women-writing-visibility`  
**Assessment:** Strong conceptual frame; needs more than one prominent example and careful oral-history attribution.  
**Decision:** Retain, strengthen scene integration and verify source passages.

### What works
- It addresses the historical record and its selection effects.
- It does not present Lady Pink as the only woman writer.
- It uses Pabón-Colón's transnational work to resist a NYC-only frame.
- It explicitly warns against inferring identities or biographies from unattributed material.

### Required changes
- Pabón-Colón's publisher record confirms the study draws on the lives of over 100 women in 23 countries. Describe this as the scope of her research, not as a representative census of all women writers.
- Lady Pink's Smithsonian Archives of American Art oral history is a first-person source. It records her account of beginning to write around 1979 and participating in the Fashion Moda exhibition at the end of 1980, when she was 16. Attribute these details to her recollection; do not present retrospective memory as an independently verified exact chronology without corroboration.
- The draft's NEA citation and Smithsonian oral history should be differentiated: one is a 2013 magazine interview, the other a later oral-history interview. Add the exact date/description of the interview and use transcript time/page markers where available.
- The sentence about being included “at age 16” is supported by her oral history. If the article discusses the “token female” framing, attribute that interpretation to Lady Pink's recollection of the organisers' attitude; do not state it as an objective institutional motive without another source.
- Add at least two further women writers or local examples in the next editorial pass, selected from well-sourced first-person or scholarly records. Do not create a token list; integrate people into their regional histories and networks.
- “Women have been part of graffiti writing since its early decades” should be supported with named early examples and not rest solely on Lady Pink plus a contemporary transnational study.
- Keep gender as a historically and socially situated dimension; do not infer identities from images or aliases.

### Source notes
- NYU Press, *Graffiti Grrlz*: https://nyupress.org/9781479895939/graffiti-grrlz/
- Smithsonian Archives of American Art, Lady Pink oral history: https://www.aaa.si.edu/collections/items/detail/lady-pink-interview-27380
- NEA interview (2013): https://www.arts.gov/stories/magazine/2013/2/ahead-their-time/pure-expression

## Entry 04 — New York subway writing: the train as a moving surface
**ID:** `history.nyc-subway-era`  
**Assessment:** Clear and useful; policy narrative must be explicitly source-framed.  
**Decision:** Retain with a policy-source note and a stronger cultural counterpoint.

### What works
- The train is explained as a moving display and communications surface.
- Doors, windows, seams and car divisions are treated as compositional conditions.
- The text correctly says May 1989 was not the end of graffiti culture.
- The photographic record is distinguished from the complete original work.

### Required changes
- The 1990 Sloan-Howitt and Kelling case study is an institutional/policy account written from the Clean Car Program perspective. Its language frames writers as offenders and presents the program as a successful management intervention. Cite it for the program's dates and stated procedure, not as a neutral account of writer culture.
- Distinguish the April 1984 announcement from the May 1984 initial deployment. The case study says the program was announced in April and began with two trains in May 1984.
- The case study identifies 12 May 1989 as the removal/cleaning of the last graffiti-covered car. Preserve this exact date if used; the headline phrase “by May 1989” is less precise.
- Add a sentence noting that the Clean Car Program's own success metric is fleet condition, not the disappearance of graffiti writers, their culture or their work on other surfaces.
- Add writer/photographer testimony or an archival source alongside the policy study so the article does not give the authority's narrative the final word.
- The statement that train architecture shaped composition is credible, but should be tied to Coetsee with exact pages or diagrams.
- Ensure “panel”, “end-to-end” and “wholecar” are explained as formats, not as universal chronological stages.

### Source notes
- Sloan-Howitt & Kelling (1990), *Security Journal* 1(3), pp. 131–136 onward: https://popcenter.asu.edu/content/subway-graffiti-new-york-city-getting-vs-meanin-it-and-cleanin-it
- PDF text confirms April 1984 announcement, May 1984 initial rollout and 12 May 1989 final removal/cleaning milestone.
- Austin, Coetsee and MCNY should provide the complementary cultural, spatial and archival perspective.

## Entry 05 — How writing travelled: media, exchange and local adaptation
**ID:** `history.global-circulation`  
**Assessment:** Good comparative idea, but current regional examples are too thin for the title's global promise.  
**Decision:** Retain as a bounded introduction; rename or qualify scope and deepen the case studies.

### What works
- It rejects a simplistic one-way diffusion model.
- Amsterdam and Paris are treated as different historical pathways.
- São Paulo pixação is explicitly not equated with New York tagging.
- It separates contact, circulation, similarity and proven influence.

### Required changes
- The Amsterdam source is Robin Vermeulen's 2015 Bachelor's thesis at the Reinwardt Academie, based on literature research and ten interviews (five writers, two graffiti aficionados and three heritage institutions). The draft currently says “ten in-depth interviews” without describing the interview mix. Correct this.
- Vermeulen describes Amsterdam graffiti resembling contemporary forms emerging around 1977, with early writers largely from punk contexts; he then distinguishes a later generation around 1983 influenced by New York through books such as *Watching My Name Go By*, *The Faith of Graffiti*, *Subway Art*, and the 1985 VPRO broadcast of *Style Wars*. The current draft compresses these phases into “a later phase influenced by American hip-hop writing” and should make the chronology clearer.
- Vermeulen also includes testimony that punk and hip-hop did not exist in sealed, mutually exclusive worlds. Avoid implying two entirely separate cultures with a clean handover.
- The Paris source, *Petite histoire du street art*, is an institutional heritage/tourism overview, not a specialist scholarly history. Its section uses “street art” broadly and offers a compact retrospective. Attribute the claims to the publication and avoid presenting it as definitive for all France.
- The Paris text specifically mentions independent radio, the TV programme H.I.P H.O.P., north-east Paris and La Plaine Saint-Denis, and 1984 events at Stalingrad and Fort d'Aubervilliers. The draft should either include the 1984 anchor events with the source's institutional status made clear or narrow its claim.
- Three regional examples do not justify a comprehensive “global circulation” chapter. Either retitle it “How writing travelled: selected European and transnational examples” or add a balanced set of documented cases from other regions in a later edition.
- If São Paulo is mentioned in the body, include a clear short explanation of pixação and link to the dedicated scene entry; otherwise remove the drive-by mention.
- Add first-person testimony from Amsterdam and Paris before adding named crews or people. Avoid treating an official city summary as the only voice.

### Source notes
- Vermeulen (2015), especially Chapter 1, pp. 10–12 and interview methodology in the abstract: https://www.ahk.nl/media/lichtingen/docs/2015/Robin_Vermeulen_-_Graffiti_in_Amsterdam.pdf
- Ville de Paris / Plaine Commune, *Petite histoire du street art* (2021), p. 1: https://cdn.paris.fr/paris/2022/01/06/c77b856c573321ba59dc5b402eb98c97.pdf
- Pereira's São Paulo study should support a distinct pixação entry, not a passing equivalence.

## Entry 06 — The blackbook: sketch space, social object and memory
**ID:** `learning.blackbook`  
**Assessment:** Strong concept and useful archive entry; Rahn's specific blackbook claims need exact text verification.  
**Decision:** Retain, but verify the ethnographic details before publication.

### What works
- The blackbook is presented as a practice, social object and archive.
- The article distinguishes sketches from photographs and finished public work.
- Rahn's situated Montreal study is explicitly not universalized.
- The Getty example demonstrates a public institutional collaboration inspired by blackbook exchange practices.

### Required changes
- Correct the Rahn bibliographic record: Janice Rahn, “Painting Without Permission: An Ethnographic Study of Hip-Hop Grafitti Culture,” *Material Culture Review* 49(1), 1999. The journal record confirms participant observation and interviews, but the abstract alone does not establish every specific blackbook anecdote in the draft. Cite exact article pages for gatherings, book exchanges, sketching and painting from memory.
- Do not label the study's period or every observation as “Montreal in the 1990s” unless the article itself gives the fieldwork dates and scope. The publication is 1999 and author affiliation is Concordia University in Montreal; those facts alone do not prove every participant or observation date.
- The Getty facts are correct but need precise phrasing: the decade-long project resulted in an artists' book with 143 works on paper; 151 artists were invited to examine the archive in 2013. The book is not simply 143 contributions from the 151 invitees unless the source says so.
- Getty explicitly connects the collaborative book to black sketchbooks circulated among graffiti artists. This is a strong institutional source for that particular project and its conceptual relationship.
- The phrase “a photograph records a particular work as it appeared in a place and moment” is useful, but a photograph's provenance, date, photographer and subject identification still require independent verification.
- Add a visual rights warning: the Getty page displays digitized works, but the archive must check item-level rights and terms before reproducing them.

### Source notes
- Rahn (1999), journal article record: https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/view/17784
- Getty Research Institute, *LA Liber Amicorum*: https://www.getty.edu/research/special_collections/notable/la_liber_amicorum/index.html
- Getty digital collection: https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/digital_collections/notable/aiseborn_liber_amicorum.html
