# BLACKBOOKS, SKETCHBOOKS AND PEER LEARNING

**Scope:** the black book as sketch space, social object, archive and learning environment.  
**Status:** research synthesis drawing on ethnography and institutional collections.

## 1. More than a notebook

A black book is not merely a place to store finished drawings. The sources document several overlapping functions:
- practicing and testing letterforms;
- developing ideas for future pieces;
- collecting tags, signatures and drawings from other writers;
- showing skill and building relationships;
- carrying a portable record of a writer’s visual development;
- preserving work that may never have existed on a durable public surface.

The exact practices vary by scene, period and individual.

## 2. Ethnographic evidence: sketching, sharing and practice

Janice Rahn’s *Painting Without Permission: An Ethnographic Study of Hip-Hop Graffiti Culture* is based on participant observation and interviews in Montreal in the 1990s. Rahn describes writers eagerly sketching and passing around black books and photographs at gatherings. She records that black books were used to sketch ideas for pieces and to have other writers sign or sketch their tags. At gatherings, writers brought them to make contacts and show skills. Some worked from a sketch while painting; others worked from memory but still used books to explore combinations and approaches. [A: ethnographic research]

Rahn also records a social distinction: some writers were respected for painting freehand without relying on a sketch at the wall. That does not make sketchbooks irrelevant; rather, the book could be a practice and experimentation space even for writers who did not use a direct visual reference while painting.

Her account is a situated study of a particular community and period. It should not be generalized to every city or generation.

## 3. Peer learning and social circulation

Rahn describes sketchbook sharing as a non-authoritative form of self-directed learning. This is important for NIGHTSHIFT’s archive: knowledge is not only passed through formal instruction. It circulates through:
- observing other writers’ pages;
- exchanging books and photographs;
- receiving signatures and drawings;
- comparing solutions to letter problems;
- building relationships and reputations.

The book is therefore both a creative tool and a social object. It records not just one person’s development but traces of a network.

## 4. Institutional example: Getty’s LA Liber Amicorum

The Getty Research Institute’s *LA Liber Amicorum* project offers a well-documented institutional example. In 2013, 151 graffiti and tattoo artists were invited to explore the Getty’s collections, including writing manuals, emblem books, sketchbooks, engravings and penwork. The project resulted in an artists’ book containing 143 works on paper. Getty explicitly connects the project to the practice of inviting crew members and other artists to inscribe black sketchbooks with lettering or drawings. [A: Getty Research Institute]

The project’s name references the historical *liber amicorum*, or “book of friends”: a book passed between people and filled with signatures, poetry and coats of arms. Getty describes the contemporary black book as a collaborative practice and the project as a collective portrait of Los Angeles artists’ individual approaches to letterforms, handstyles, symbols and signs.

This is a specific curated project, not proof that every black book follows the same format or has the same social function.

## 5. Archiving the ephemeral

The University of Hamburg’s CSMC project on Hamburg writing notes that photography is central to making ephemeral public writing tangible, archivable and reconstructable. It studies DAIM’s private archive, including approximately 50,000 photographs, books, magazines, sketchbooks and correspondence. [A: university research project]

Black books and photographs preserve different kinds of evidence:
- black books preserve intentional paper-based experiments and social inscriptions;
- photographs preserve works as they appeared in a particular place and moment;
- correspondence and magazines document networks and circulation;
- oral histories preserve memories and interpretations, with the limitations of retrospective testimony.

No single archive is complete. The disappearance of a wall piece does not mean no evidence survives; the survival of a photograph does not automatically establish full context or exact date.

## 6. Archive record proposal

**Canonical term:** Black book  
**Aliases:** blackbook; sketchbook; piecebook (usage varies)  
**Category:** Practice / object / social archive  
**Functions:** sketching, experimentation, exchange, signatures, peer learning, documentation  
**Related terms:** handstyle, piece, crew, writer network, photograph, magazine, archive  
**Evidence:** ethnographic study and Getty institutional documentation  
**Editorial caution:** preserve local and individual differences; do not define a single mandatory format.

## 7. NIGHTSHIFT design inference

A blackbook system should behave like a working studio and a social archive, not just a gallery of saved outputs.

Possible features:
1. **Process pages:** save rough sketches, alternate letter constructions and abandoned variants.
2. **Version history:** show how a word changes through revisions.
3. **Page inscriptions:** fictional in-world writers can leave original, non-imitative contributions.
4. **Contact/network layer:** connect pages to crews, events and relationships.
5. **Reference notes:** attach dates, locations, materials and source confidence to historical examples.
6. **Personal archive:** allow players to curate and revisit their own work.

For historical figures, do not fabricate real signatures or attribute invented pages to actual writers. In-game collaborative pages should use fictional characters and original art.
