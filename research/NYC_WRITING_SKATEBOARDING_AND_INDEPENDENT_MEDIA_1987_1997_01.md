# NYC Writing, Skateboarding and Independent Media 1987–1997 — Research Dossier 05

**Scope:** a wider scene map of New York's graffiti, skateboarding, hip-hop and independent media networks from the late 1980s through the 1990s. This dossier complements the hardcore crossover files. It does not claim that these scenes were one unified community.

## 1. The convergence was spatial and social, not a single organization

Jeremy Elkin's documentary *All the Streets Are Silent: The Convergence of Hip Hop and Skateboarding (1987–1997)* frames downtown Manhattan in the late 1980s and early 1990s as a meeting ground for skateboarding and hip-hop. Its official description identifies a convergence that shaped a distinctive visual language and names participants from music, skateboarding and media.

Eli Gesner's first-person account in *The Standard* describes 1987 as a period when he skateboarded, wrote graffiti and spent much of his time outdoors with friends. In the same interview, he describes later club and party connections through Tunnel and Funkmaster Flex's Wednesday party. This is an individual recollection, not proof that every downtown skater or writer belonged to one network.

**Evidence distinction:** the documentary is a retrospective synthesis built from interviews and archival footage; Gesner's interview is practitioner recollection. Both support overlapping social spaces and people, but neither establishes a formal crew called “the downtown scene.”

## 2. Brooklyn Banks: skate spot, meeting point and urban stage

Anthony Pappalardo's 2017 interview with photographer Ari Marcopoulos describes the Brooklyn Banks as a major New York skate spot and a meeting point from which skaters ventured elsewhere. Marcopoulos recalls the site as a place where skaters, graffiti artists and homeless New Yorkers shared an urban environment.

This supports the Banks as a spatial node and meeting place, not a claim that all graffiti writers painted there or that it was controlled by one crew.

A 2025 TransWorld Skateboarding video interview with Zoo York co-founder Eli Morgan Gesner revisits the Banks through his memories of 1980s New York. It is a later oral-history source and useful for locating further first-person recollections, but it should not replace period photographs or footage.

## 3. Zoo York: a bridge between New York skate identity and graphic culture

Dazed's 2013 first-person essay by Eli Gesner recalls that he became interested in graffiti writing in 1982, while older people in his Upper West Side neighborhood were remnants of the original 1970s Zoo York skateboard/graffiti crew. He describes moving from writing into skateboarding through that local peer group.

A 1999 TransWorld Skateboarding profile reports that Rodney Smith, Eli Gesner and Adam Schatz founded Zoo York in 1993, aiming to establish a lasting New York skate organization rather than remain a feeder team for West Coast companies.

The two sources establish a distinction:
- the earlier Zoo York identity is recalled as a 1970s skateboarding/graffiti crew;
- the 1993 Zoo York company was a later organizational and commercial project founded by Smith, Gesner and Schatz.

Do not collapse the historical crew, the 1993 company and later brand ownership into one unchanging entity. The exact continuity of membership and naming requires more primary documentation.

## 4. Skateboarding and graffiti: direct crossover evidence

The NYHC/graffiti feature in i-D identifies FCEE as an original member of Chaka Malik's Stagedive skateboarding team in the late 1980s and describes the fusion of graffiti, hardcore and skating. This is a specific named link, rather than a broad assumption that skaters and writers naturally overlapped.

The same feature documents SMOG's attendance at early CBGB hardcore matinees and use of a Crumbsuckers character in his graffiti. Combined with the Stagedive account, it demonstrates at least some direct person-level links among writing, skateboarding and hardcore.

**Limit:** these named cases do not justify treating all NYC skate crews as graffiti crews, or all graffiti writers as skaters.

## 5. Mixtape and video circulation as a bridge

The official *All the Streets Are Silent* materials identify Eli Gesner's earlier *Mixtape* video as a key precursor. A festival press-kit PDF describes it as combining freestyle footage of hip-hop performers with skate footage of Harold Hunter, Jeff Pang, Danny Supa, Robbie Gangemi and Peter Bici. Gesner recalls that he initially combined Stretch and Bobbito radio-show tapes with skate footage for a skate audience; the tape unexpectedly gained wider attention and sold through its first run.

This is a useful example of cross-scene circulation through a physical medium: skate shops and VHS tapes could carry hip-hop performances into skate culture and vice versa. The film's archival synthesis is retrospective; the quoted recollection is attributed to Gesner.

## 6. Graffiti zines: the post-train circulation network

Greg Lamarche's first-person introduction, published by Letterform Archive in 2023, recalls starting to write in 1981 and creating *Skills*. The zine ran seven issues from 1992 to 1995 and received submissions from writers around the world. Lamarche describes the late-1980s shift toward photographing work before it was removed and exchanging photographs by mail, with images then cut and pasted into independent zines.

The Letterform Archive's *Subscription to Mischief* exhibition describes the mid-1990s zine ecosystem as a response to the reduced presence of painted subway trains and the spread of photocopiers and personal computers. It characterizes zines as regional reports and analog precursors to a global writing community.

This complements the Queens hardcore fanzine research:
- hardcore zines such as *The Village Noize* and *Yes Zista* focused on bands and local music networks;
- graffiti zines such as *Skills* circulated tags, throw-ups and pieces through photo exchange;
- both used inexpensive print technologies and personal networks, but their editorial subjects and contributor networks should remain distinct unless an individual or artifact proves overlap.

## 7. A cautious chronology

| Period | Supported development | Evidence caution |
|---|---|---|
| 1987–1989 | Downtown skate / hip-hop convergence; graffiti writing continues while train access and policy pressures change; photo exchange becomes increasingly important | Do not treat 1989 as the end of all NYC writing |
| 1990–1993 | Skate-video, club, radio and print networks connect people and images; Zoo York company founded in 1993 | Distinguish local crew identity from later company |
| 1992–1995 | *Skills* publishes seven issues and receives international submissions | The archive includes material gathered for an eighth issue that never appeared |
| 1995–1997 | Independent zines, video and photography continue to circulate urban youth culture; *Mixtape* reaches skate audiences | Do not infer one unified subculture or a single media channel |

## 8. NIGHTSHIFT design implications — inference, not history

1. **Represent places as nodes, not ownership territories.** Brooklyn Banks, CBGB matinees, downtown clubs, skate shops, train yards and print shops can host overlapping but non-identical networks.
2. **Give each medium its own circulation rules.** VHS moves through skate shops and friends; graffiti photos move through mail exchanges and zines; flyers move through venues and hand-to-hand distribution.
3. **Track people across roles over time.** A person may be a writer, skater, photographer, designer, promoter or musician in different combinations and periods.
4. **Keep organizations distinct.** A crew, skate team, company, magazine and venue are different entity types even when the same people connect them.
5. **Make the 1989 transition a shift in infrastructure, not an ending.** Wall writing, freight trains, photography, zines and other surfaces continue beyond the subway-cleaning milestone.
6. **Use fictional media artifacts in gameplay.** Historical zines, logos, footage, signatures and album artwork remain archive references; do not reproduce them as in-game assets without rights.
