# NYC Graffiti Zine and Media Network, 1990–1999 — Research Dossier 01

**Document type:** Media history / archive research  
**Status:** Source-backed synthesis; publication inventory remains incomplete  
**Scope:** New York City and its national/international exchange networks, 1990–1999  
**NIGHTSHIFT use:** Blackbook Archive, zine system, reputation, discovery, scene communication and historical media props.

## 1. Main finding

The 1990s graffiti zine was not merely a printed gallery of finished pieces. It was a social and documentary infrastructure. Photographs (“flicks”), handstyles, sketches, letters, envelopes, stickers, flyers and editorial choices connected writers who might never meet in person.

Letterform Archive's exhibition on 1990s graffiti zines describes a transition from mail-based exchanges to small-run publications and later online forums. Its archival material includes original pages and correspondence. Greg Lamarche's first-person account explains how *Skills* grew from a handmade publication into a widely distributed magazine, while preserving the labor and personal relationships behind each issue.

## 2. Skills (1992–1995): a documented case study

The Letterform Archive exhibition records that *Skills* ran for seven issues between 1992 and 1995. Greg Lamarche assembled layouts by hand, cutting and arranging photographs before photocopying or scanning them. The magazine accepted contributions from writers in major cities and smaller towns, and showed a broad range of writing: tags, throw-ups, productions, walls and freights.

The distribution history is unusually specific:
- Issue 1: approximately 100 copies.
- Tower Records began distributing the magazine in 1993.
- Issue 7: print run of approximately 10,000 copies.
- Submissions continued arriving at Lamarche's PO box well into 1997.
- Material gathered for a planned eighth issue survives in the archive.

Lamarche's retrospective essay adds personal context: he began *Skills* after graduating college in 1991, using access to a print shop in the Boston area; he moved back to New York in July 1995. This distinction matters: *Skills* is strongly tied to a Queens-born New York writer and New York networks, but its early production was in the Boston area. Do not mislabel the magazine as exclusively produced in Queens.

## 3. A broader zine ecosystem

The Letterform Archive exhibition and Joe Austin's historical research identify a varied ecology of 1990s graffiti publications. Titles and roles include:

- **Skills:** broad visual coverage from handstyles to productions, walls to freights; international submissions.
- **Styles for Miles (SFM):** outline/sketch-focused publication with a teaching and style-study orientation. Joe Austin describes it as a Manhattan writer's zine from 1990, intended to be copied and studied by developing writers.
- **Move:** later publication associated with the same Manhattan writer behind SFM; exact issue chronology needs further bibliographic work.
- **Graf News, Flashbacks, Graphic Scenes, X-plicit Language:** identified by Austin as next-generation New York writers' zines. Full issue-level metadata remains to be collected.
- **IGTimes / International Get-Hip Times:** an earlier zine whose name and format evolved; Austin notes its explicitly political editorial perspective.
- **G.S.E.L.:** identified by Lamarche as a source of rare photographs from around New York City.
- **Twelve Ounce Prophet:** Lamarche recalls its strong following and later transformation into an early online graffiti forum.
- **Videograf:** graffiti publication translated into VHS/video form; named collaborators include San 2, Kool Spin, William Green / Nic One and Sacha Jenkins in Lamarche's recollection.
- **Hip Hop Tribe (Tribe):** Italian publication noted by Lamarche for its large format and wrap-around full-bleed cover art; demonstrates international circulation into New York's media networks.

This is a starter list, not a complete catalogue. Some titles began before the 1990s or continued beyond it; issue dates and publisher identities must be verified individually.

## 4. The mechanics of the mail network

Lamarche describes contributors mailing photographs and material to editors. In *Skills*, the number of submitted flicks was counted and a matching number returned, an explicit fair-trade practice. The archive preserves illustrated envelopes, letters, stickers, sketches and handwritten notes.

This gives NIGHTSHIFT a historically grounded system:
- A writer submits a photo or sketch.
- An editor chooses what to publish.
- A printed issue reaches shops and other writers.
- Readers copy, study, discuss or respond to the material.
- Correspondence and repeat submissions build relationships over time.
- A zine's editorial identity affects which forms and regions become visible.

Do not reduce this to an abstract “social media feed.” The delay, cost, physical handling, scarcity and editorial selection are central to the period.

## 5. From print to online

The archive material documents submissions continuing through 1997. Lamarche recalls *Twelve Ounce Prophet* later becoming one of the early online graffiti forums. This suggests a gradual overlap between mail, print and digital communication rather than an overnight replacement.

NIGHTSHIFT should distinguish:
- physical flick exchange,
- zine submission and editorial selection,
- retail distribution,
- video circulation,
- early web/forum participation.

The exact launch dates and technical history of the relevant online forums require separate research before attaching precise years.

## 6. Brooklyn as a physical media and painting node: BEDT

The Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal (BEDT) waterfront property in Williamsburg/Greenpoint provides a separate but related case study of abandoned industrial space and graffiti production.

A 2016 LTVSquad history reports that the terminal went bankrupt in 1983 and that, by the late 1980s, abandoned buildings were being used by graffiti writers. It identifies a 1990s photograph by Lady Pink and names writers associated with the site, including Sane/Smith, Lady Pink, Cycle, Muze, Natz, Hush, Rebel, Vor and DBI Crew. The article notes that different writers used different names for parts of the property (“paint warehouses,” “Sports crew” warehouses and other local labels).

This is a retrospective specialist account, not a contemporaneous inventory. It supports the existence of an important Brooklyn waterfront painting location and a diverse group of users, but does not establish that all named writers painted there in the same year or belonged to one crew.

The site's industrial geography, abandonment and multiple informal names make it a strong research lead for NIGHTSHIFT's Brooklyn environment. Do not reproduce its access methods or treat dangerous abandoned property as a safe playground.

## 7. Correction: SANE/SMITH and the Brooklyn Bridge date

Earlier research summaries repeated a 2017 i-D article's wording that placed the famous SANE/SMITH Brooklyn Bridge production in 1990. A 2019 Complex interview with Roger Smith explicitly states that he and Sane painted the Manhattan tower of the Brooklyn Bridge in **1988**. The same interview distinguishes the initial 1988 work from a later return to paint the bridge again after the civil dispute.

For NIGHTSHIFT chronology, use 1988 for the famous first Brooklyn Bridge production, based on Smith's direct recollection in the 2019 interview. Retain the 2017 i-D date as a conflicting secondary account, not as the preferred date. Sane died in October 1990 according to the i-D account. Do not conflate the 1988 bridge production with the 1990 death or later bridge activity.

## 8. NIGHTSHIFT design implications

1. **Make the archive physical.** Letters, envelopes, stamps, photocopies, contact sheets, stickers, folded zines and VHS sleeves can form a coherent interface.
2. **Editorial selection matters.** A zine is not an automatic mirror of the whole scene; editors shape what is visible.
3. **Represent reciprocity.** Mail exchanges can involve sending flicks and receiving an equivalent number in return.
4. **Preserve delays and scarcity.** Print cycles, postal delivery, limited print runs and shop distribution should feel different from instant digital feeds.
5. **Let style knowledge travel.** Sketch-oriented titles such as SFM can support studying outlines and letter structures without copying any real writer's signature.
6. **Keep regional and international exchange.** Skills' submissions and international mail demonstrate that local scenes were already connected globally before social media.
7. **Separate media node from crew.** A zine, archive, store or forum can connect writers without being a crew itself.
8. **Use original fictional publication brands in the game.** Historical titles belong in the research archive; game-world zines should use original names and designs unless licensed.

**Research conclusion:** The 1990s zine system was a distributed, reciprocal archive built from flicks, letters, editorial selection, print shops, record stores, VHS and later forums. For NIGHTSHIFT, it is a historically grounded social system in its own right—not just collectible decoration.
