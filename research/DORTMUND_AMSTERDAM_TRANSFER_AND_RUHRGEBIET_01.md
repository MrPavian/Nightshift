# Dortmund–Amsterdam and the Ruhrgebiet — Transfer, parallel development and regional evidence 01

**Date:** 2026-11-15  
**Status:** research dossier; synthesis, not a definitive style history  
**Purpose:** examine claims about Amsterdam's influence on early Dortmund writing while separating parallel local histories, transnational circulation, practitioner testimony and later scholarly interpretation.

## Executive finding

The available evidence supports three propositions of different strength:

1. **Amsterdam had a substantial graffiti/tagging culture before New York-style hip-hop writing became widely adopted in Europe.** The Dutch Graffiti Library's account of *Punk Graffiti Archives: The Netherlands* describes a punk- and squatter-linked scene from the late 1970s, followed by a second generation around 1980 that pursued citywide visibility. It says the scene was transformed as New York writing circulated through books and documentaries in the mid-1980s. This is a retrospective archive/library account based on a 2018 publication, not a census of all Dutch writing.
2. **Dortmund had an identifiable local writing scene by the mid-1980s, documented contemporaneously in *Da kukse wa* (1987).** The author page identifies Hubert Moormann's photographs and interviews with CHINTZ, SHARK, CHANA and ZODIAK. The book's local Writer-ABC records period-specific terms such as “getting fame”, “tag”, “piece” and “whole car”.
3. **A later scholarly regional account connects SHARK's visit to Amsterdam in the mid-1980s with an influence on SHARK, ZODIAK, CHINTZ, CHANA and others.** The KWI essay by Florian Deckers makes this claim in its discussion of Dortmund. It is an important research lead, but the exact mechanism, dates, objects seen, and which visual or social practices were transmitted are not established by the accessible passage alone.

The evidence does **not** support a simple one-way story in which Amsterdam invented Dortmund writing, nor does it establish a single transferable “Amsterdam style” or a fixed Dortmund style grammar.

## 1. Amsterdam before and during the New York-writing circulation

### 1.1 A locally rooted pre-history

The Dutch Graffiti Library's *Punk Graffiti Archives: The Netherlands* page describes the Amsterdam scene as developing from the late 1970s among punk and squatter youth. It distinguishes an early phase of punk-linked slogans, band names and nicknames from a second generation that pushed citywide visibility and tagging more intensively. The page says the Amsterdam scene flourished until New York-style graffiti was imported and widely adopted in the mid-1980s.

The same page describes the Dutch publication *Punk Graffiti Archives: The Netherlands* (Urbanario, 2018; 164 pages, 194 illustrations) as based on private archives, including the Diana Ozon Archive and Dutch Graffiti Library, and reproducing period newspaper and fanzine material. This is a strong route into primary-period artifacts, but the online article is still a retrospective summary; individual artifacts must be inspected before making detailed visual claims.

Source:
- Dutch Graffiti Library, “Punk Graffiti Archives: The Netherlands” (2019): https://dutch-graffiti-library.nl/stories/punk-graffiti-archives-the-netherlands/

### 1.2 Oral-history and publication routes

The Dutch Graffiti Library's *AMSTERDAM ON TOUR* description presents a book about Amsterdam's late-1970s/early-1980s scene, with more than 30 participant accounts and interviews including N-Power, Hugo Kaagman, EGO, SHOE, WALKING JOINT, AGAIN, GASP, HIGH, YAKI KORNBLIT, BLADE, RHYME, DELTA, CAT22 and JAZ. It is a valuable acquisition/consultation lead for the pre-1985 Amsterdam context.

Source:
- Dutch Graffiti Library, “AMSTERDAM ON TOUR” (2019): https://dutch-graffiti-library.nl/stories/amsterdamontour/

Robin Vermeulen's 2015 Reinwardt Academie thesis, *Graffiti in Amsterdam: De vergankelijkheid van een eigentijdse cultuur*, is an accessible academic heritage study based on literature and ten in-depth interviews, including five graffiti writers across generations and Amsterdam heritage institutions. Its focus is preservation and heritage, not a definitive formal history, but it provides a documented oral-history method and a substantial bibliography/interview trail.

Source:
- Robin Vermeulen (2015), Reinwardt Academie thesis: https://www.ahk.nl/media/lichtingen/docs/2015/Robin_Vermeulen_-_Graffiti_in_Amsterdam.pdf

## 2. Dortmund: contemporary evidence and the later influence claim

### 2.1 What the 1987 source actually establishes

Marianne Brentzel's author page identifies *Da kukse wa — Dortmunder Graffiti* as a 1987 publication with photographs by Hubert Moormann and interviews with CHINTZ, SHARK, CHANA and ZODIAK. The page reproduces the local Writer-ABC and a contemporary framing of Dortmund's writing activity.

The Writer-ABC defines:
- black-book as a book for sketches and photographs of painted and not-yet-painted works;
- character as a comic-like sprayed figure;
- “getting fame” as the writer's goal of becoming known;
- piece as a large graffiti picture;
- tag as the written name;
- whole car as a completely painted train.

These are attributed local definitions from a particular publication and moment. They should not be silently modernized or presented as universal definitions.

Sources:
- Marianne Brentzel, *Da kukse wa* bibliographic page: https://www.mariannebrentzel.de/da-kukse-wa.html
- Marianne Brentzel, excerpt and Writer-ABC: https://www.mariannebrentzel.de/da-kukse-wa-leseprobe.html

### 2.2 The Amsterdam visit claim

Florian Deckers's KWI essay states that Dortmund writers CHINTZ, ZODIAK and SHARK were TUF members and that a mid-1980s Amsterdam visit by SHARK brought influences from Amsterdam's “bombing mentality and style” to SHARK and other Dortmund writers. The accessible essay passage establishes that this is a published scholarly interpretation. It does not, by itself, establish:
- the exact date or duration of the visit;
- who travelled with SHARK;
- which Amsterdam writers, locations or works were encountered;
- whether the influence concerned frequency/visibility, naming practices, handstyle, letter construction, crew/social organization, or a combination;
- whether the effect was direct, mediated through photographs/publications, or retrospectively remembered;
- how the influence varied between CHINTZ, ZODIAK, CHANA, SHARK and other writers.

These questions require the underlying source cited by Deckers and, ideally, first-person testimony from the people involved. Do not convert the KWI sentence into a precise visual recipe.

Source:
- Florian Deckers, “Graffiti im Ruhrgebiet”, KWI Blog / Pixelprojekt+City Scripts (22 June 2022): https://blog.kulturwissenschaften.de/graffiti-im-ruhrgebiet/

### 2.3 Contemporary press is evidence of reputation, not neutral style analysis

The 1989 *DIE ZEIT* report describes SHARK, CHINTZ and CHANA as prominent figures and refers to a locally recognized “Dortmund style”. It is useful evidence of public visibility and contemporary reputation. It is a journalistic account embedded in a prosecution/media controversy, not a neutral technical analysis of letterforms or a comprehensive account of the regional scene.

Source:
- *DIE ZEIT*, “Graffiti-Prozeß: Helden der Szene” (1989): https://www.zeit.de/1989/42/helden-der-szene/seite-2

## 3. A more accurate model: parallel roots, later exchange

For NIGHTSHIFT, model the relationship as a set of testable connections rather than a single origin chain:

- **Amsterdam, late 1970s–early 1980s:** locally rooted punk/squatter tagging and citywide visibility practices; distinct from, but later interacting with, New York-style writing.
- **New York / transatlantic media circulation, early–mid-1980s:** books, films and other media help circulate a more extensive writing culture across Europe. The exact local reception varies.
- **Dortmund, mid-1980s:** a local hip-hop/writing scene and TUF network become visible in contemporary documentation.
- **Amsterdam–Dortmund contact, mid-1980s (reported):** SHARK's visit is identified by Deckers as a transmission route; its precise content remains to be verified.
- **Dortmund and wider Ruhrgebiet, late 1980s onward:** local forms, reputations and practices circulate regionally and internationally; the scale and direction of this circulation require city-specific evidence.

This model allows for local invention, parallel development, borrowing, adaptation and feedback. It avoids treating “influence” as proof that a city copied another city's visual style.

## 4. What can be translated into NIGHTSHIFT — and what cannot

### Supported as research/education content

- Explain that Amsterdam had a pre-existing local tagging history before the broad adoption of New York-style writing.
- Explain that Dortmund's 1987 publication documents a distinct local scene and vocabulary.
- Present the Amsterdam visit as a sourced but incompletely specified influence claim, with attribution to Deckers.
- Distinguish practice/visibility (“bombing” in the KWI account) from lettering form and individual handstyle.
- Teach circulation as a process involving travel, observation, photography, publications, films, peer networks and adaptation.

### Not supported yet

- A named “Amsterdam handstyle preset”.
- A fixed “Dortmund style” parameter set or universal block-letter rule.
- Claims that all named Dortmund writers adopted the same features.
- A claim that Amsterdam's earlier punk tagging was simply the same practice as later New York-derived writing.
- Any claim about stroke order, writing speed or exact tool use from static photographs.
- Copying or training on identifiable writer signatures or book photographs without item-specific rights clearance.

## 7. Primary-source follow-up — SHARK's Amsterdam visit (15 November 2026)

A second source pass located a more explicit published account of the visit, but not yet its underlying first-person record.

### 7.1 What the publication says

The Hitzerot shop/publisher description for *ALL SURFACE – Tagging in Dortmund* states that SHARK had described visiting Amsterdam in the mid-1980s, seeing what a completely “bombed” city looked like, and wanting to do the same in his hometown. The description then says that the mentality as well as the style of the Dutch capital became a major influence on SHARK and a named group of Dortmund writers (ZODIAK, CHINTZ, CHANA, SATYR, ZHIT, SPOOK, KINOR and ATAIR). This is more specific than the KWI summary because it distinguishes the remembered citywide condition and the stated ambition to reproduce it in Dortmund.

**Evidence grade:** published retrospective account attributed to SHARK by the *ALL SURFACE* publisher/retailer description. The page does not identify the original interview, recording, exact quotation context or date of the testimony. It is therefore not yet a directly inspectable primary-source transcript. Do not label it a verified verbatim quotation or treat the retailer copy as the book's complete editorial apparatus.

Source: Hitzerot, *ALL SURFACE – Tagging in Dortmund*, product description, https://hitzerot.com/shop/all-surface/ (accessed 15 November 2026), especially the paragraph on SHARK's Amsterdam visit. The same description identifies the book as published November 2022 by Holger Wiese and Tobias Sawitzki and gives 244 pages.

### 7.2 Independent context, not corroboration of the visit

Mark Gmehling's 2015 interview recalls Dortmund as a place where graffiti began to grow in 1984 and names Chintz, Cole, Shark and Zhit as early heroes; he says he moved from skateboarding to spray painting around 1988 after seeing their graffiti grow. This is a first-person recollection by a later participant and is useful for the local scene's visibility and generational reception. It does **not** independently corroborate SHARK's Amsterdam visit or specify the visual content of the influence.

Source: Sami Wakim, “Interview: Mark Gmehling”, Street Art United States, 13 March 2015, https://streetartunitedstates.com/interview-mark-gmehling/ (accessed 15 November 2026), answer to the opening question.

### 7.3 KWI footnote check

The KWI essay's note 4 points to Markus Wiese (2002), *Iron City: Graffiti Dortmund* (MASmedia), for a detailed illustrated history of Dortmund graffiti from its beginnings in the 1980s to the early 2000s. It does **not** cite a distinct interview or source in that footnote for the Amsterdam visit. Note 5 cites Lisa Gottlieb (2008) for a systematic classification of graffiti styles; that reference supports neither the Amsterdam travel anecdote nor a Dortmund-specific formal grammar. The Amsterdam claim therefore still needs its underlying source located.

Source: Florian Deckers, “Graffiti im Ruhrgebiet”, KWI Blog, 22 June 2022, reference notes 4–5, https://blog.kulturwissenschaften.de/graffiti-im-ruhrgebiet/ .

### 7.4 Updated evidence assessment

- **Supported:** a retrospective publication description attributes to SHARK a mid-1980s Amsterdam visit and a stated ambition to bring the observed citywide bombing intensity to Dortmund.
- **Supported as a separate local recollection:** Mark Gmehling remembers the growth and visibility of Dortmund graffiti from 1984 and identifies early local figures.
- **Not yet established:** the original SHARK interview, exact words, date/location of visit, specific Amsterdam writers or sites encountered, and which particular visual/technical traits were adopted by each Dortmund writer.
- **Not justified:** a direct “Amsterdam style” preset, a universal Dortmund block-letter recipe, or a claim that every named writer received the same influence.
