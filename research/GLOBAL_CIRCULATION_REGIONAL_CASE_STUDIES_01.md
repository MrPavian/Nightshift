# Global Circulation — Regional Case Studies 01

**Document type:** Comparative regional research brief  
**Status:** Source-backed synthesis; selected cases, not a complete world history  
**Scope:** Amsterdam, Paris / Seine-Saint-Denis, São Paulo and selected Chinese cities  
**Purpose:** Explain how graffiti writing circulated internationally while local histories, media and visual systems shaped distinct outcomes.

## 1. Editorial finding

The international history of graffiti writing is not a simple story in which New York invented a finished style and the rest of the world copied it. New York's writing culture became globally influential through films, books, photography, hip-hop music, travel and personal exchange. But in several places, local forms of public writing already existed or developed through different social and cultural routes.

The selected cases demonstrate four different relationships:
- **Amsterdam:** a punk- and squatter-linked name-writing culture existed before New York-style hip-hop writing became widely known locally.
- **Paris / Seine-Saint-Denis:** imported hip-hop culture was received through French media, radio and television, then developed in a distinctive metropolitan geography shaped by the relationship between Paris and its banlieues.
- **São Paulo:** pixação developed a highly distinctive local writing practice and visual language; scholarship warns against flattening local categories into a simple graffiti-versus-pichação binary.
- **China:** contemporary graffiti developed through different urban centres, international contacts and local cultural reinterpretation; Beijing, Shanghai and Chengdu should not be collapsed into one national scene.

These are not four equivalent national summaries. Each case is a specific, source-led example of circulation, adaptation and local agency.

## 2. Amsterdam — punk writing before the hip-hop wave

### 2.1 What the sources support

Robin Vermeulen's 2015 Reinwardt Academie graduation research describes an Amsterdam name-writing culture emerging around 1977, largely among young people connected to punk. It identifies names including VENDEX, OZON, DE ZOOT, N-POWER, THE DUMB, AMARILLO and DR. RAT. The study is based on literature and ten in-depth interviews, including five graffiti writers from different generations and three heritage institutions.

The thesis distinguishes this early Amsterdam culture from the later arrival of American hip-hop writing. It reports that films and books about U.S. graffiti inspired a new local style and that Amsterdam hip-hop graffiti developed as a recognisably different phase. The thesis also records a local scene concern: the preservation of graffiti history should be led by writers and their communities rather than imposed solely by formal institutions.

The Dutch Graffiti Library's Punk Graffiti Archives project provides further visual and editorial context. It describes Amsterdam's late-1970s punk and squatter tagging, followed by a second generation that pursued city-wide visibility. It also compares Amsterdam with Madrid's flechero tradition and argues that these local systems were partly displaced when New York-style writing became more widely known through books and documentaries.

### 2.2 Why this matters

Amsterdam is a strong example of **parallel development followed by cultural exchange**. It shows that name-writing, public visibility and youth subculture were not waiting for imported New York imagery to exist. The later adoption of tags, throw-ups, pieces and train writing did not erase the earlier punk context from the city's history.

## 3. Paris and Seine-Saint-Denis — media, banlieues and urban geography

### 3.1 What the sources support

The City of Paris / Plaine Commune publication Petite histoire du street art describes the 1980s spread of hip-hop culture in France through independent radio and the television programme H.I.P H.O.P. It locates early crew activity in north-east Paris and the industrial wastelands of La Plaine Saint-Denis. It identifies the 1984 block parties at Stalingrad and the first French hip-hop festival at the Fort d'Aubervilliers industrial site as important moments in the consolidation of these territories within French hip-hop history.

David Fieni's chapter “Graffiti and Street Art in Paris” frames Paris and its suburbs as one of the major centres of urban graffiti and street art from the early 1980s. It stresses the geography and demographics of Paris proper in relation to the banlieues, including populations shaped by postcolonial migration, as important context for the development of local practices.

This is a distinct history from the older graffiti traditions of Paris. Elizabeth Sage's peer-reviewed study of fin-de-siècle “jaytalking” documents an earlier Parisian culture of public writing in the late nineteenth century. That history is not modern hip-hop writing, but it demonstrates why “graffiti began in New York” is an unacceptable statement about all public inscription.

### 3.2 Why this matters

The Paris case is not simply “New York style arrived in France”. Media distribution, radio, television, hip-hop events, industrial spaces, migration and the social geography of the banlieues shaped the local scene. The wider Paris region matters; a city-centre-only account would miss essential context.

## 4. São Paulo — pixação as a local writing system

### 4.1 What the sources support

Research on São Paulo describes pixação as a locally developed form of public name-writing that became visible around 1980 and spread substantially during the 1990s and 2000s. A peer-reviewed article in Novos Estudos / CEBRAP describes its characteristic graphic language as vertically elongated letters built from straight lines and pointed forms, often called “tag reto”. The article notes competing explanations for the style's visual references, including the city's tall buildings and lettering associated with heavy metal and punk records. These are presented as interpretations, not settled origins.

The same research describes pixação as distinct from more readily assimilated forms of colourful mural graffiti. It is associated with public inscription, group identity, spatial visibility and a particular relationship to the urban skyline.

Pedro Moreira Graça's 2017 University of São Paulo dissertation, O Muro de fora, examines the multiple strands of mural painting and writing in São Paulo from the 1970s onward. Its abstract explicitly argues that the city's different groups have their own roots, visual examples, rules and systems of internal valuation. It traces their references and contacts with analogous practices elsewhere.

A 2019 article by Marcos Zibordi challenges the reductive division between graffiti and pixação as two sealed-off practices, arguing that their processes and visual forms can overlap.

### 4.2 Why this matters

São Paulo demonstrates **local formal invention and contested categories**. Pixação should not be described as a Brazilian version of a New York tag, nor should it be reduced to “black letters on buildings”. Its typographic language, social meanings, local vocabulary and relationship to the city's architecture require their own entry and sources.

## 5. China — multiple centres and local reinterpretation

### 5.1 What the sources support

Graffiti in China, edited by Adriana Iezzi and published by the University of Bologna's 1088press in 2024, is an unusually useful research source. The book is based on research in Beijing, Shanghai and Chengdu and includes interviews with artists and crews. Its editors explicitly state that these cases are selected and cannot represent the entire Chinese scene.

The book describes three different urban contexts:
- **Beijing:** political and administrative centre, with strong state presence; includes the Beijing Penzi (BJPZ), the Kwanyin Clan and ABS crew.
- **Shanghai:** international and commercial city, with a scene shaped by cosmopolitan exchange; includes Oops Crew and the work of Tin.G, associated with the China Graffiti Girls.
- **Chengdu:** a different regional centre, demonstrating that graffiti development cannot be reduced to Beijing and Shanghai alone.

The book also documents artists seeking a Chinese visual language through references to Chinese characters, calligraphy, traditional painting and literature. It records international travel and exchange, including crews working with foreign writers.

Its chapter on the arrival of writing in Europe notes the importance of films such as Wild Style and Style Wars, books, tours and fanzines in the international circulation of writing. That chapter should be treated as a scholarly synthesis with references, not as a complete timeline for every European country.

### 5.2 Why this matters

China demonstrates that global circulation does not mean cultural erasure. Imported writing practices can be reinterpreted through local scripts, visual traditions, urban conditions and institutions. It also shows why a single “Chinese graffiti style” would be misleading.

## 7. Cross-case conclusions for the Archive

1. **Use “circulation”, not “export”, as the default explanatory model.** Export can be used for specific media routes, but it should not imply passive recipients.
2. **Record pre-existing local writing.** A local scene may have developed before New York-style writing became known there.
3. **Separate media from practice.** A film, book, magazine, radio programme or visit is a channel of transmission, not itself the scene.
4. **Map local terminology.** Preserve terms such as pixação, tag reto, wicked, whip and local punk-writing vocabularies with their geographic and historical scope.
5. **Do not equate graffiti writing with street art.** They intersect, but mural commissions, stencil practices, political writing and name-centred writing have different histories.
6. **Treat regional chapters as authored histories.** Do not build a country checklist with one shallow paragraph per nation.
7. **Preserve contested and overlapping categories.** The same city can contain multiple traditions and people who move between them.
8. **Keep image rights separate from historical evidence.** A scholarly image or archival photograph is not automatically reusable in the game.

## 8. Priority follow-up research

1. Amsterdam: verify early punk-writing chronology against primary zines, period press and oral histories; identify exact dates and locations for early writers.
2. Paris: deepen the 1980s chronology using interviews, period magazines, TV/radio archives and local scene histories; distinguish Paris from Seine-Saint-Denis and other banlieues.
3. São Paulo: read the USP dissertation and peer-reviewed pixação research in full; establish local vocabulary and differences between pixação, pichação and graffiti as used by practitioners.
4. China: use the 2024 University of Bologna volume as a launch source; add interviews and primary documentation for each selected city and crew.
5. Europe: build separate case dossiers for London, Berlin, Basel, Düsseldorf and Munich only where sources add a distinct chronology or practice.
6. Media routes: create a dated matrix for Style Wars, Wild Style, Subway Art, Spraycan Art, fanzines, foreign writer visits and local hip-hop broadcasts, avoiding assumptions that every city received them at the same time.
