# NYC Graffiti, Skateboarding and Alternative Media Networks 01

**Scope:** research dossier on the overlap and differences between New York graffiti writing, skateboarding, hip-hop and independent media from the late 1970s through the 1990s. It complements the NYC writer/crew and hardcore crossover dossiers.

**Editorial distinctions:** academic interpretation, practitioner recollection, contemporary journalism, archival institutional framing and NIGHTSHIFT design inference are kept separate. Shared participants or visual language do not imply that the scenes were one unified movement.

## 1. The subway as a communication network

Abram Coetsee's Cornell University dissertation, *New York Subway Style Writing: Aesthetics, Infrastructure and Technology* (2022), argues that writers transformed the subway transportation system into a communication technology. The abstract situates this within city infrastructure, uneven urban development and social disinvestment, and examines the sociability and aesthetic methods through which writers used the system.

In a section on PHASE 2 and the IGTimes, Coetsee discusses PHASE 2's recollection of a citywide network spanning Manhattan, Brooklyn and the Bronx. The subway lines were not merely transport to painting locations: their routes allowed writers to encounter names, compare styles, meet people and circulate ideas. The dissertation quotes PHASE 2 describing the 2, 4 and 5 lines as especially important connections among boroughs.

Coetsee also discusses the Writer's Bench as a meeting and exchange point where writers watched passing trains, met, made plans, signed blackbooks and settled disputes. This is a specific social infrastructure, not just a viewing spot.

**Evidence type:** scholarly synthesis drawing on attributed practitioner recollections and archival research. The quotations should remain attributed to PHASE 2 and the dissertation's interpretation should not be presented as a direct statement by all writers.

## 2. The transition from train visibility to archival circulation

The Cornell dissertation's chapter on PHASE 2 and IGTimes describes the 1984–1994 publication as a transnational network for style writing. It traces how photographs and printed pages became auxiliary spaces for writing after the subway's visibility declined. It also discusses the way photocopies and publications helped circulate styles beyond New York, connecting regional and international scenes.

The Letterform Archive's *Subscription to Mischief* exhibition gives a complementary institutional account:
- By the mid-1990s, most bombed trains had been removed from service and penalties had become harsher.
- Writers adopted photocopiers and personal computers to produce small-run zines.
- Publications received mailed submissions from writers around the world.
- Graffiti magazines became a means for practitioners to document their own culture and functioned as regional field reports.

Greg Lamarche's 2023 first-person account adds a specific example:
- He began writing in Queens in 1981.
- His zine *Skills* ran for seven issues between 1992 and 1995.
- It received submissions from writers around the world.
- His archive includes letters, envelopes, flyers and photographs, including material gathered for an eighth issue that was never published.

**Assessment:** together these sources support a shift from the train as a moving public display toward a distributed print-and-photo network. This is not a clean replacement: trains, walls, freight, photography, blackbooks and print overlapped.

## 3. Skateboarding and graffiti: a documented but not universal overlap

In a 2021 interview connected to the documentary *All the Streets Are Silent*, Eli Gesner describes New York in 1987 as a period when a close-knit group of approximately 40 young people combined skateboarding, graffiti and hip-hop. He recalls skating throughout the city, writing graffiti and spending time outdoors with friends. He says the group was known simply as “The Skaters” and that he experienced hostility from police and other street groups.

Gesner co-founded Zoo York in 1993. In the interview he describes the later rise of New York skateboarding, with *Kids* (1995), Zoo York and the 1997 Zoo York Mixtape helping make NYC a fixture in the skateboard world.

**Evidence type:** retrospective first-person recollection, published by The Standard in 2021. The “around 40 kids” figure and the claim that the mix was unique to this group are Gesner's own characterization, not an independently verified census or proof that all members wrote graffiti.

**Design caution:** do not project the later commercial success of Zoo York back onto the whole 1987 scene. The interview itself describes a transition from a marginalized local group to later cultural visibility.

## 4. Hardcore and graffiti: parallel scenes, not one seamless subculture

The 2016 Harsh Forms investigation into the graffiti/hardcore crossover is especially useful because it documents the limits of reconstructing a stable network:
- The author says documentation was scarce and that the network was assembled using Freddy Alva's research, at149st.com, photographs and first-hand help from Alva and SMOG.
- The author explicitly warns that a crew diagram cannot accurately represent the fluidity of affiliations, name changes, crew mutations, disputes and changing membership.
- SMOG describes graffiti and hardcore as “parallel lines,” comparing the relationship to playing football on Saturdays and attending matinees on Sundays. In his account, people in one scene did not necessarily care about the other.

This is a crucial counterweight to the visual evidence of graffiti on hardcore covers, flyers and stickers. Artwork and shared friends can demonstrate contact, but do not establish that the two scenes merged into one organization or identity.

## 5. The 1989 transition: continuity rather than disappearance

Adam Gopnik's contemporary *New Yorker* article “Buffed Out” (19 February 1990) describes the removal of the last graffiti-covered subway car from service in May 1989 and the resulting post-subway context. It reports that writers gathered at Henry Chalfant's Grand Street studio to draw, exchange information and meet visitors. It names A-One, Crash, Daze, Dondi and Lee among those present and describes their attempts to establish gallery and museum careers.

Greg Lamarche's later recollection in Letterform Archive likewise says that after the subway era's end in 1989, bombed trains continued to circulate through worldwide photo exchange among practitioners, enthusiasts and street photographers.

**Synthesis:** 1989 marks a major institutional and material transition for NYC subway writing, not the end of writing. The social and visual practice continued through walls, freight, studios, photography, blackbooks, zines, exhibitions and later digital networks.

## 7. NIGHTSHIFT design implications — inference, not historical fact

1. Model the subway as a network layer: routes, stations, yards, viewing points and borough links can affect who encounters whose work and where information travels. Do not reduce it to a fast-travel map.
2. Model social exchange separately from crew membership: seeing a piece, meeting at a bench, signing a blackbook, exchanging photographs and joining a crew are distinct event types.
3. Represent media migration as overlapping channels: train, wall, freight, photograph, blackbook, zine, studio, gallery and digital archive. Avoid a simplistic “subway ends, zines begin” switch.
4. Give zines a production workflow: submissions, editing, photocopying, layout, printing, mailing and archive preservation. A publication can connect cities without making its contributors members of one crew.
5. Treat skateboarding as a parallel scene with intersection points—shared individuals, locations, photography, clothing and video—not as a universal attribute of NYC writers.
6. Preserve source perspective. A participant's estimate of a 40-person group is an attributed memory, not a fixed database count.
7. In gameplay, fictionalize names, logos and graphic identities. Historical evidence can inform systems and atmosphere without reproducing protected marks or identifiable artwork.
