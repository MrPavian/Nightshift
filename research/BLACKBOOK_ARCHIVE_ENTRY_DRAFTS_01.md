## Entry 01 — The emergence of modern name-writing

- **Proposed ID:** `history.modern-name-writing`
- **Primary type:** History / period
- **Facets:** Philadelphia; New York City; late 1960s–early 1970s; name-writing; handstyle; youth culture
- **Evidence state:** Strong overall; individual city chronologies require their own qualifications
- **Editorial state:** Draft
- **Visual rights:** No image cleared; use an original timeline/map or link to institutional collections

### Short description

Modern graffiti writing did not begin with one person, one city or one universally agreed date. During the 1960s and early 1970s, young people in several American cities developed increasingly visible practices of writing names and nicknames in public space. Philadelphia and New York are both important to this early history, but their scenes did not develop as identical versions of the same thing.

### Main text

The word *graffiti* describes many different forms of public inscription. This entry focuses more narrowly on modern name-centred writing: the practice of repeatedly writing an assumed name or nickname, developing a recognisable personal hand and building visibility among peers.

Philadelphia and New York belong near the beginning of this history. Research on Philadelphia documents name-writing activity from the mid-1960s, including the work of CORNBREAD, COOL EARL and TOPCAT 126. New York's writing culture developed through its own neighbourhood networks and became especially visible through the city's subway system during the 1970s. The available evidence supports a history of related early centres, not a neat story of one city inventing the practice for everyone else.

In New York, the 1971 newspaper coverage of TAKI 183 helped bring name-writing to a much wider public. That moment matters as a media event; it is not proof that one person invented graffiti writing. Press visibility, the emergence of a practice and the later development of a scene are different historical questions.

Over time, writers developed increasingly differentiated handstyles, larger letter compositions and shared vocabularies for practices and formats. These changes were not a universal ladder that every writer or city followed. Local histories, peer networks, surfaces, media and individual decisions shaped different outcomes.

### What this entry does not claim

- It does not claim that modern name-writing is the origin of all graffiti or public inscription.
- It does not name a single inventor.
- It does not treat Philadelphia as a footnote to New York or New York as a copy of Philadelphia.
- It does not claim that every city followed the same sequence of tags, throw-ups and pieces.

## Entry 02 — Philadelphia: name-writing and local lettering

- **Proposed ID:** `scene.philadelphia`
- **Primary type:** Place / scene
- **Facets:** Philadelphia; 1960s onward; name-writing; handstyle; WICKED/WICKET; local terminology
- **Evidence state:** Strong for the broad scene outline; medium for some transmission/influence claims
- **Editorial state:** Draft
- **Visual rights:** No image cleared; create an original schematic of tall-hand proportions or link to rights-cleared institutional material

### Short description

Philadelphia is one of the important early centres of modern name-writing in the United States. Its history includes a local vocabulary and lettering traditions that should not be treated as unfinished versions of New York styles.

### Main text

During the 1960s, young people in Philadelphia wrote names and nicknames across neighbourhood streets and transit corridors. CORNBREAD became the best-known figure in later public accounts, but he was part of a wider local culture that also included writers such as COOL EARL and TOPCAT 126. Research traces the growth of a city-wide scene through the second half of the decade. The evidence does not support reducing this history to one inventor or one uncontested “first tag”.

Philadelphia and New York developed related but distinct writing cultures. Philadelphia's local traditions include elongated handstyles, tall hand or tall print, and the strongly scene-specific WICKED/WICKET tradition. These forms are not simply early attempts at New York lettering. Their proportions, spatial use and insider vocabulary belong to a particular local history.

Some research connects TOPCAT 126's lettering to later New York terminology such as “Broadway Elegant” or “Manhattan Style”. This is an important possible point of exchange between scenes, but the exact route and degree of influence should be presented as an attributed interpretation, not as a fully documented chain of transmission.

The Philadelphia case changes the wider story. It reminds us that the history of modern writing is not a single line radiating from one city. Different local scenes formed their own visual languages, and those languages met, travelled and changed through people and media.

### Local terminology note

“Gangster Hand”, “Tall Hand”, “Tall Print”, “Wicked” and “Wicket” should not be collapsed into one universal style taxonomy. The terms appear in particular source and community contexts and may describe overlapping aspects of letterform, scale, movement or local identity. Preserve attribution and variants.

### What this entry does not claim

- It does not claim CORNBREAD was the first graffiti writer in the world.
- It does not claim every Philadelphia writer used the same naming or lettering conventions.
- It does not state as settled fact that TOPCAT 126 directly caused a specific New York style.
- It does not reduce WICKED/WICKET to a fixed generator alphabet.

## Entry 03 — Women in the writing scene: participation, visibility and the archive

- **Proposed ID:** `history.women-writing-visibility`
- **Primary type:** History / cultural actors
- **Facets:** New York; transnational networks; gender; documentation; oral history; archival omission
- **Evidence state:** Strong for the general argument; individual early biographies remain unevenly documented
- **Editorial state:** Draft
- **Visual rights:** Do not use images without item-level permission; prioritize first-person interview links and original explanatory graphics

### Short description

Women have been part of graffiti writing since its early decades. The historical problem is not an absence of participation, but an uneven record of who was named, photographed, interviewed, collected and remembered.

### Main text

Graffiti writing is often imagined as a boys' club. That image has shaped both popular memory and parts of the written history. It can make women's participation seem exceptional or recent, even when research and first-person accounts show women taking part in early scenes and in later international networks.

Jessica Nydia Pabón-Colón's *Graffiti Grrlz: Performing Feminism in the Hip Hop Diaspora* draws on the lives of more than 100 women in 23 countries. Rather than treating women as isolated exceptions, the book examines communities, peer networks, digital connections and the ways women writers reshape the culture around them. Its transnational scope also warns against treating one New York story as the template for every place.

Lady Pink is one of the most visible early New York examples. In a 2013 National Endowment for the Arts interview, she recalled discovering graffiti in 1979, painting subway cars and being included at age 16 in the 1980 Fashion MODA exhibition. A later Smithsonian Archives of American Art oral-history interview provides a further first-person record of her recollections about beginning to paint trains and entering the Fashion MODA exhibition. These sources document her own account; they should not be stretched into a claim that she was the first woman to write graffiti.

The archive must also resist a second simplification: adding one celebrated woman to an otherwise unchanged male chronology. Women belong in the ordinary histories of crews, scenes, blackbooks, exhibitions, styles, photography and networks. At the same time, names found in early books or retrospective lists are not automatically complete biographies. For figures such as Barbara 62, Eva 62 and Stoney, the archive should seek direct testimony and specific historical records before publishing detailed profiles.

A responsible archive records both what is known and how the record was made. It distinguishes a writer's own testimony from a later scholar's interpretation, a photograph from an identified maker, and historical absence from proof that someone was not there.

### What this entry does not claim

- It does not claim that every scene offered women the same opportunities or barriers.
- It does not treat Lady Pink as the sole representative of women writers.
- It does not infer a person's identity, crew membership or career dates from an unattributed photograph or passing mention.
- It does not turn the absence of records into proof of absence.
