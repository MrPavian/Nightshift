## Entry 04 — New York subway writing: the train as a moving surface

- **Proposed ID:** `history.nyc-subway-era`
- **Type:** History / period
- **Facets:** New York; 1970s–1989; subway; train composition; documentation; public policy
- **Evidence:** Strong for policy chronology and institutional collections; individual experiences require attributed testimony.
- **Editorial state:** Draft

### Short description
The New York subway was more than a large painting surface. Trains carried writing across neighbourhoods and through a shared transit network, changing who could see a piece and how it circulated.

### Main text
During the 1970s, New York writers increasingly used subway cars as moving displays. A train could carry a name or a large composition far beyond the place where it had been painted. Its route created an audience across boroughs, while repeated movement made the subway a distinctive form of public visibility.

The work changed in scale and structure over time. Tags and smaller signatures existed alongside more elaborate pieces. Writers developed compositions that occupied panels and, in some cases, much larger portions of a train. The train itself mattered: doors, windows, seams and the division between cars shaped the available visual field. A photograph of one side or one moment cannot always show the complete work or its sequence.

The subway also became the focus of an increasingly organized campaign to remove writing from the transit fleet. New York's Clean Car Program began in 1984. Its stated approach was to clean newly tagged or renovated cars quickly or remove them from service. A 1990 case study by Maryalice Sloan-Howitt and George L. Kelling reported that by May 1989 the program had achieved its goal of a graffiti-free fleet. The Museum of the City of New York likewise describes the subway as officially declared graffiti-free in spring 1989.

That date is a major turning point in the history of New York subway writing. It is not the end date of graffiti culture. Writing continued on other surfaces and through blackbooks, photography, magazines, films, exhibitions and international networks. The subway campaign changed the conditions around one important format; it did not erase the people, practices or visual knowledge that had developed around it.

### What this entry does not claim
- It does not claim every writer painted trains or that every work followed the same format.
- It does not equate a photograph with the complete original piece.
- It does not treat the Clean Car Program's institutional success measure as proof that graffiti culture ended.
- It does not imply every train everywhere was permanently free of graffiti after May 1989.

## Entry 05 — How writing travelled: media, exchange and local adaptation

- **Proposed ID:** `history.global-circulation`
- **Type:** History / cultural circulation
- **Facets:** New York; Amsterdam; Paris / Seine-Saint-Denis; film; books; photography; radio/television; local scenes
- **Evidence:** Strong for the bounded case studies below; not a complete global history.
- **Editorial state:** Draft

### Short description
Graffiti writing travelled through films, books, photographs, music, travel and personal exchange. What arrived in another city was not simply copied: local histories and social conditions shaped what happened next.

### Main text
New York writing became internationally visible through photography, books, films, hip-hop music, exhibitions and personal contact. These channels helped people encounter lettering and practices they might never have seen in person. But circulation is not uniform adoption. A book or film could introduce visual ideas while local writers interpreted them through their own streets, histories, languages and networks.

Amsterdam offers a clear example of different phases meeting. Research by Robin Vermeulen describes a local name-writing culture emerging around 1977 among young people connected to punk. His study draws on ten in-depth interviews as well as literature and heritage institutions. It distinguishes this earlier local culture from a later phase influenced by American hip-hop writing, including films and books. The point is not that Amsterdam had one unchanging tradition; it is that New York-style writing entered a place where public name-writing and youth subcultures already had a history.

The Paris region followed another route. The City of Paris and Plaine Commune's historical publication describes the spread of hip-hop in France during the 1980s through independent radio and television, including the programme H.I.P H.O.P. It connects early activity to north-eastern Paris and industrial spaces in La Plaine Saint-Denis, and identifies events in 1984 as important moments in the consolidation of French hip-hop territories. The geography of Paris and its banlieues is essential; a story limited to central Paris would miss much of the context.

These examples are not interchangeable. Amsterdam's documented punk-linked name-writing and Paris's 1980s hip-hop reception show different relationships between local practice and international circulation. São Paulo's pixação, for example, has a distinct local vocabulary and visual system and should not be treated as a local spelling of New York tagging.

A global history therefore needs more than arrows pointing outward from New York. It needs to ask what travelled, through which channels, who encountered it, what local practices already existed, and how writers changed what they received.

### What this entry does not claim
- It does not claim New York was the origin of all graffiti or public name-writing.
- It does not claim Amsterdam's punk-linked writing and later hip-hop writing were identical.
- It does not reduce the Paris region to a single city-centre scene.
- It does not present these cases as a complete survey of every country.
- It does not use “influence” where sources establish only contact, circulation or similarity.

## Entry 06 — The blackbook: sketch space, social object and memory

- **Proposed ID:** `learning.blackbook`
- **Type:** Practice / object / learning
- **Facets:** sketching; handstyle; pieces; exchange; peer learning; documentation; archive
- **Evidence:** Strong for documented examples; practice varies by person, place and generation.
- **Editorial state:** Draft

### Short description
A blackbook is more than a notebook of finished drawings. It can be a place to test letters, develop ideas, exchange sketches, build relationships and preserve traces of a writing network.

### Main text
A blackbook can hold rough experiments, developed compositions, tags, drawings and inscriptions by other writers. It is both a working space and a social object. Its pages may show how a letter changes, but they can also record who met whom, who exchanged drawings and which ideas circulated through a community.

Janice Rahn's ethnographic study of a Montreal graffiti community in the 1990s describes writers bringing blackbooks and photographs to gatherings. They used the books to sketch ideas for pieces, exchange signatures and drawings, show their skills and make contacts. Some writers worked from sketches when painting; others preferred to paint from memory but still used books to explore combinations and approaches. Rahn's research is valuable precisely because it describes a particular community and period. It should not be turned into a rule for every writer.

The Getty Research Institute's *LA Liber Amicorum* project offers a different, institutional example. In 2013, 151 graffiti and tattoo artists were invited to explore Getty collections and contribute to an artists' book containing 143 works on paper. Getty connects the project to the practice of inviting crew members and other artists to add lettering or drawings to black sketchbooks. The project's name refers to the historical *liber amicorum*, or book of friends: a volume circulated among people and filled with inscriptions.

Blackbooks preserve a different kind of record from photographs. A sketchbook can document intention, experimentation and social exchange. A photograph records a particular work as it appeared in a place and moment. Neither is complete on its own. The disappearance of a wall piece does not mean no evidence survives, and the survival of a photograph does not automatically establish the maker, date or full context.

The blackbook is therefore part studio, part correspondence and part archive. It gives a view of writing culture that cannot be understood by looking only at finished pieces on walls or trains.

### What this entry does not claim
- It does not prescribe one mandatory blackbook format.
- It does not assume every writer sketches before painting.
- It does not treat Rahn's Montreal fieldwork as representative of every scene.
- It does not treat a sketch as proof that the corresponding public piece was painted.
- It does not authorize copying real writers' pages, signatures or unpublished work.
